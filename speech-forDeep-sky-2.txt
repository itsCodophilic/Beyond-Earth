On the fourth of July ten fifty-four, astronomers in China recorded a guest star in Taurus, near the star Zeta Tauri. It was so bright that it could be seen in daylight for twenty-three days, and at night for almost two years. Six and a half thousand light-years away, a star about ten times the mass of the Sun had run out of fuel. Its core collapsed, in less than a second, into a neutron star, and the rest of the star was blasted into space. Nearly a thousand years later, that debris is still flying outward at about fifteen hundred kilometres a second. It is the Crab Nebula, about eleven light-years across, its filaments glowing in red and orange, its centre a blue haze lit by the neutron star, which still spins thirty times every second. This is the Crab Nebula today.

On the twenty-third of February nineteen eighty-seven, deep underground in Japan, the United States and Russia, detectors caught about two dozen neutrinos in thirteen seconds. Hours later, a new star appeared in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy beside our own, a hundred and sixty-eight thousand light-years away. Supernova nineteen eighty-seven A was the nearest seen in almost four hundred years, and the first whose neutrinos were caught. Its star, a blue supergiant called Sanduleak minus sixty-nine two hundred and two, about twenty times the mass of the Sun, had collapsed. The neutrinos escaped at once; the light followed hours later. Around it are three rings of gas the star shed twenty thousand years before. Since two thousand and one, the debris has been crashing into the inner ring, lighting it up like a string of pearls. In twenty twenty-four, the Webb telescope found signs of the neutron star at its heart.

On the twenty-seventh of December twenty-oh-four, a flash of gamma rays swamped detectors on spacecraft all over the Solar System. It came from SGR eighteen oh six minus twenty, a magnetar on the far side of our galaxy, tens of thousands of light-years away. A magnetar is a neutron star, about twenty kilometres across, with the strongest magnetic field known, a thousand trillion times Earth's. That field strains the star's solid crust until it cracks: a starquake. The field snaps into a new shape, and in a fifth of a second the magnetar released more energy than the Sun gives out in a hundred thousand years. A cloud of fireball stayed trapped in its field, pulsing every seven and a half seconds as the star turned. Even from so far away, its gamma rays disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere.

On the fourteenth of September twenty fifteen, both LIGO detectors, three thousand kilometres apart, felt the same tiny shiver in space, a stretch smaller than a thousandth of the width of a proton. A billion and three hundred million years earlier, two black holes, thirty-six and twenty-nine times the mass of the Sun, had been circling each other. Black holes bend light: the stars behind them appear smeared into rings. As they circled, they stirred space itself, sending out gravitational waves, and lost energy, and drew closer. In the last fifth of a second they whirled around dozens of times, and merged into one black hole of sixty-two Suns. The missing three Suns' worth of mass went out as gravitational waves. It was the first time anyone had felt them, a century after Einstein predicted them.

Our nearest star after the Sun is Proxima Centauri, four point two four light-years away, a small, cool red dwarf, a seventh the width of the Sun. Around it orbits a rocky planet, Proxima b, twenty times closer to its star than Earth is to the Sun. On the eighteenth of March twenty sixteen, Proxima flared. A tangle of its magnetic field snapped, and in minutes the star became sixty-eight times brighter, bright enough, for a moment, to see with the naked eye. Red dwarfs like Proxima are churned right through by convection, which tangles their fields, so their flares are far fiercer than our Sun's for their size. Our Sun's biggest flares brighten it by less than one percent. A blast like Proxima's would scour its planet, and flares like it, several times a year, could strip away any ozone layer it might have.

Betelgeuse, the red star at Orion's shoulder, is one of the biggest stars we can see, about five hundred and fifty light-years away. Put where the Sun is, its surface would reach past the orbit of Mars, toward Jupiter. In the winter of twenty nineteen and twenty twenty, it faded to about a third of its usual light, and people wondered if it was about to explode. It was not. In twenty nineteen, its boiling surface threw off a huge bubble of gas from its southern half. As the gas moved away, it cooled, and condensed into dust, which blocked part of the star from our view. By April it had recovered. Betelgeuse will explode one day, probably in about a hundred thousand years. From Earth, it will shine as brightly as a half Moon for weeks, visible in daylight. But it is far enough away that it cannot harm us. A supernova would have to be within about fifty light-years to damage Earth's ozone layer.

Beside the Northern Crown, between the bright stars Arcturus and Vega, is a star too faint to see without a telescope. It is T Coronae Borealis, about three thousand light-years away. In May eighteen sixty-six, it suddenly became as bright as the North Star. In February nineteen forty-six, eighty years later, it did it again. It is a recurrent nova: a white dwarf, circling a red giant every two hundred and twenty-eight days, collecting its gas, and every eighty years or so, exploding. Before nineteen forty-six it dimmed a little, then brightened. Since twenty fifteen, it has been doing the same again, so astronomers think the next eruption is due any time. When it comes, it will last only a few days. Look beside the Northern Crown.

On the ninth of October twenty twenty-two, the brightest burst of gamma rays ever recorded hit Earth. Astronomers called it the BOAT: the brightest of all time. It came from a galaxy about two point four billion light-years away, in the direction of Sagitta. A massive, fast-spinning star had run out of fuel and collapsed straight into a black hole. Gas swirling into it launched two jets at almost the speed of light, which drilled out through the star, and one of them was pointed at Earth. Even after two billion years of travel, its gamma rays disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere, and overwhelmed many of the instruments that caught them. A burst as bright as this one may come along only once in ten thousand years.

In September twenty nineteen, a sky survey saw the centre of a galaxy about two hundred and fifteen million light-years away suddenly brighten. A star like the Sun had strayed too close to the black hole at its heart, a black hole of about a million Suns. Near a black hole like that, the pull on a star's near side is much stronger than on its far side. Close enough, that difference beats the star's own gravity. The star was pulled into a long stream of gas: astronomers say spaghettified. About half of the gas flew off into space. The other half fell back, swirled into a hot disc, and blazed for months. This event, AT twenty nineteen q i z, is one of the best watched of these tidal disruptions.

Toward the constellation of Sagittarius, behind thick clouds of dust, lies the centre of our galaxy, twenty-six thousand seven hundred light-years away. Rise above the Milky Way, and dive into its heart. There, stars race round something unseen. One, called S two, swings round it every sixteen years, at up to seven thousand six hundred and fifty kilometres a second. To pull a star like that, the unseen thing must weigh four point three million Suns, packed into a space smaller than our Solar System. It is a black hole: Sagittarius A star. In twenty twenty-two, the Event Horizon Telescope photographed its shadow, ringed by glowing gas. It is starved, swallowing only a trickle, but about once a day, a clump of hot gas circling close to the edge flares up, in under an hour. For proving it was there, Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel shared the twenty twenty Nobel Prize in Physics.

Radio telescopes sometimes catch flashes that last just a thousandth of a second, and give out as much energy as the Sun does in days. These fast radio bursts arrive about a thousand times a day, from all over the sky, almost all from other galaxies, billions of light-years away. What makes them? On the twenty-eighth of April twenty twenty, one came from inside our own galaxy, from a magnetar called SGR nineteen thirty-five plus twenty-one fifty-four, about thirty thousand light-years away. Its magnetic field strained and snapped, and with a burst of X-rays came a millisecond flash of radio waves. On the way to Earth, the thin gas between the stars slows the lower radio frequencies, so a burst arrives as a sweep, high notes first. How long the sweep is tells how far the burst has travelled.

On a dark night, in the constellation of Andromeda, you can see a faint smudge. It is the Andromeda Galaxy, a trillion stars, two and a half million light-years away: the most distant thing you can see with the naked eye. And it is coming toward us, at about a hundred and ten kilometres a second. For years, astronomers expected a head-on collision in about four and a half billion years. Now, with better measurements and the pull of the neighbouring galaxies included, it looks like a coin toss: about a fifty percent chance that the two merge within ten billion years. If they do, their stars will almost never collide; they are too far apart. Instead, the two spirals will swing through each other, fling out long tails of stars, and settle into one great elliptical galaxy.
