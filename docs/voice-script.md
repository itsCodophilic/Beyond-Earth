## Tool and settings

- **Use a text-to-speech tool, not Suno.** Suno makes songs, so its voices sing rather than speak.
- **Voice:** a calm, warm female narrator, slow pace. If the tool has a stability or calmness slider, set it high.
- **The pauses are part of the timing.** Keep the "…" and the paragraph breaks exactly as written. If the tool supports pause tags, put a 2-second break between paragraphs in Voice 2.
- **Export:** MP3 or WAV, named `voice-1.mp3` and `voice-2.mp3`, saved in `public/audio/`.

| Time | On screen | Paragraph |

```
Before there were stars… before there was time… everything we can see was packed into a space smaller than an atom.

Every galaxy. Every world. Every atom inside you. All of it, together, in one unimaginably hot, dense beginning.

This is Beyond Earth. A journey from the first instant of the universe… to the star we call home.

Look closely. Somewhere in the dark, it is waiting.

That point of light is where it all begins. When you are ready… touch it.
```

## Voice 2: the journey (revised in round 67)

Each paragraph is spoken over the scene whose **title is on screen**. It says the same thing as that scene's captions and uses the same figure as the **distance readout**. Leave a **2-second silence after every paragraph**.

| # | Title on screen | Scene length | Readout on screen | Paragraph begins |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Big Bang | 12 s | — | "The Big Bang…" |
| 2 | Where It Stopped | 8 s | — | "But in patches…" |
| 3 | The Multiverse | 14 s | — | "Everywhere else…" |
| 4 | Our Universe | 22 s | *The edge of the observable universe · 46.5 billion light-years* | "One of them is ours…" |
| 5 | The Cosmic Web | 22 s | billions of light-years, falling | "Matter never spread out evenly…" |
| 6 | Laniakea | 22 s | hundreds of millions, falling | "Laniakea…" |
| 7 | The Milky Way | 22 s | about 2.5 million (Andromeda's distance) | "From two and a half million light-years out…" |
| 8 | The Orion Arm | 22 s | about 100,000, falling | "The Orion Arm…" |
| 9 | Our Star | 14 s | about 440, then 25, then 10 | "Our Star…" |
| 10 | arrival | — | — | "Welcome… beyond Earth." |

```
The Big Bang. Not an explosion… there was nowhere for it to explode into. Space itself began to stretch, everywhere at once.

But in patches, the stretching stopped… and each patch cooled into a universe.

Everywhere else, it may still be going on… making more universes, each with its own stars… perhaps its own laws of physics.

One of them is ours. Thirteen point eight billion years old… and yet its edge is forty-six and a half billion light-years away. Space kept stretching while the light was on its way.

Matter never spread out evenly. It fell into threads… the cosmic web. Every knot is a cluster of thousands of galaxies… and between the threads lie the emptiest places that exist.

Laniakea. Our home supercluster. A hundred thousand galaxies, all falling toward the same place. Spirals, starbursts, collisions… no two of them alike.

From two and a half million light-years out… as far away as Andromeda… the Milky Way. A barred spiral, a hundred thousand light-years across, its arms wound around a bar of ancient stars. Ours.

The Orion Arm. Twenty-six thousand light-years from the centre, on a minor arm. The disc is a thousand light-years thick… and we are dropping into it. Nowhere special… and yet, home.

Our Star. A yellow, middle-aged star, four point six billion years old… holding almost all the mass of everything around it. The Sun.

Welcome… beyond Earth.
```

### Why 13.8 and 46.5 are both right

- **13.8 billion years** is the universe's **age**: how long ago the Big Bang was.
- **46.5 billion light-years** is the **distance to the edge** of the part we can see, measured today, in every direction. The 93 billion light-years the old caption gave is the same region **across**, edge to edge. That is why it was confusing next to the readout, and the caption now says 46.5 as well.
- **Why more than 13.8:** the oldest light we see left its source 13.8 billion years ago. Space kept stretching while that light travelled, so the source is now much further away than 13.8 billion light-years.

## Voice 3: when you go somewhere (round 69)

Voice 3 is what she says **when you go to something**. Click a body, open an explainer, play a space event, switch to ultraviolet or open the galactocentric view, and she says its few lines. **Jump somewhere else and she stops at once.** The next place starts its own lines. She never talks continuously. A body gets one to three short sentences, about 10–20 seconds. A view the visitor sits and watches gets four to eight sentences, about 30–45 seconds: an explainer, a space event, or the galactocentric view (recording 3F).

The text below is generated from `src/js/audio/narrationScript.js`, the same file the site reads. The words on screen and the words she speaks are therefore always the same.

### How to record it

- **Paste-ready text is in `docs/voice3-text/`.** There are 25 files, each holding only the words she speaks, one entry per paragraph, with no tags. Every one is well under ElevenLabs' 10,000-character limit. The long recording 3F is split into three files of about 7,000 characters each, and the others are under 2,900. Paste one file, generate it, and save the audio under the file's name: `Part 3F-1.txt` becomes `Part 3F-1.mp3`, and so on, in `public/audio/`. (`Part 3 (all).txt` is a reference copy of everything, too long to paste.)
- **Same voice and settings as Parts 1 and 2.**
- **No pause tags.** ElevenLabs ignored `<break>` tags here, which is likely with Eleven v3: the speech came out continuous. They are not needed. The site finds each entry from the natural pauses between sentences, matched against this text, which is how Part 2 was cut. Leaving them out also saves about 11% of the characters.
- **Read the entries in exactly this order.** The order is how the site knows which cut belongs to which body.
- **Read only the quoted text**, not the labels in bold.
- Numbers are already written out as words ("four point six billion", "twenty twenty-nine"), so the voice reads them naturally.

### Part 3L-1

- **scale-planets-between** — Here is a fact that sounds impossible. Line up every other planet, Mercury to Neptune, edge to edge, and together they measure about three hundred and eighty thousand kilometres. That is almost exactly the distance to the Moon. At its average distance they overshoot by a whisker, but when the Moon swings out to its farthest, they all fit, with room to spare.
- **scale-thirty-earths** — In most pictures, the Moon looks like it is just next door. It is not. You could line up about thirty Earths, side by side, in the gap between us and the Moon. Light crosses that gap in just over a second. The Apollo astronauts took three days.
- **scale-earths-in-sun** — This is the Sun next to the Earth, drawn to scale. Earth is that tiny blue dot, and the Sun is a hundred and nine Earths wide. But fill it up, and it could hold about one point three million Earths. The Sun alone holds almost all the mass of the Solar System.
- **scale-great-red-spot** — Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a single storm, wider than the whole Earth. It has been raging for at least two hundred years, with winds faster than four hundred kilometres an hour. But it is shrinking. A hundred and forty years ago, three Earths could have fitted across it. Today, only about one and a third.
- **scale-saturn-floats** — Saturn is almost ten Earths wide, and yet it is so light for its size that, in a big enough bathtub, it would float. It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the lightest things there are, so its average density is less than water's. Earth, made of rock and iron, would sink straight to the bottom.
- **scale-venus-day-year** — On Venus, a day is longer than a year. It takes two hundred and twenty-five Earth days to go once around the Sun, but two hundred and forty-three to turn once on its axis. And it spins backwards, so there the Sun rises in the west. From one sunrise to the next takes about a hundred and seventeen Earth days.
- **scale-olympus-everest** — This is Everest, almost nine kilometres high, the tallest mountain on Earth. And this is Olympus Mons, on Mars: about twenty-two kilometres high, two and a half Everests, and as wide as France. Mars has no moving plates, so its volcanoes sat over the same hot spot for ages, and just kept growing.
- **scale-valles-marineris** — Mars has a canyon that would stretch right across the United States. Valles Marineris is about four thousand kilometres long, and in places seven kilometres deep. Our Grand Canyon would fit inside one of its side branches. It was not carved by a river. As Mars's giant volcanoes swelled up beside it, the ground split open.
- **scale-moon-receding** — The Moon is slowly drifting away from us, about four centimetres every year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. We know, because astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and we bounce lasers off them. The tides the Moon raises on Earth pull it gently outward, and slow our spin. Long ago, a day on Earth lasted only about eighteen hours.
- **scale-light-travel** — When you look at the Sun, you are seeing the past. Its light takes eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. To reach Jupiter, it takes forty-three minutes, and to reach Neptune, more than four hours. Watch one flash leave the Sun, and race outward past every planet.
- **scale-photon-escape** — The light on your face left the Sun eight minutes ago. But that is only the end of its journey. It was born deep in the core, then spent tens of thousands of years bouncing its way out, absorbed and thrown off again in random directions, over and over. Some of today's sunlight began that trip before the first farms on Earth.
- **scale-jupiter-balance** — Jupiter is the heavyweight. Put it on one side of a cosmic balance, and every other planet on the other side. Jupiter still wins. It is two and a half times heavier than all of them combined, because it formed first, and grabbed most of the gas left over from the birth of the Sun.
- **sun-flare** — A solar flare is a sudden flash of light on the Sun. Twisted magnetic fields above a group of sunspots snap and reconnect, releasing in minutes as much energy as billions of nuclear bombs. Its light reaches Earth in eight minutes, before anything else, and the biggest flares can black out radio on the day side of the planet.
- **sun-carrington** — In September eighteen fifty-nine, the biggest solar storm on record struck Earth. The night before, the astronomer Richard Carrington had watched a brilliant flare on the Sun. Less than a day later, auroras lit up skies near the tropics, bright enough to read by, and telegraph wires sparked and carried messages with their batteries unplugged.
- **sun-gannon-storm** — In May twenty twenty-four, a burst of eruptions from one huge group of sunspots hit Earth one after another. It was the strongest geomagnetic storm in more than twenty years. Auroras were photographed as far south as India, Mexico and the Canary Islands, and millions of people saw the northern lights for the first time in their lives.
- **sun-cycle** — The Sun has a heartbeat about eleven years long. At solar minimum it is calm and almost spotless. At solar maximum it is covered in sunspots, flares and eruptions. And at every maximum, its magnetic north and south poles swap places. The cycle that began in twenty nineteen peaked around the end of twenty twenty-four.
- **sun-prominence** — A prominence is a giant loop of glowing gas, many times taller than the Earth, held up above the Sun's surface by its magnetic field. Some hang quietly for weeks. Others become unstable and erupt, flinging billions of tonnes of plasma out into space.
- **sun-coronal-hole** — Coronal holes are dark patches in the Sun's outer atmosphere. Here the magnetic field does not loop back down, but opens straight out into space, so hot gas escapes freely, as the fastest solar wind, at around eight hundred kilometres a second. When one faces Earth, auroras often follow a few days later.
- **sun-differential-rotation** — The Sun is not solid, so it does not turn as one piece. Its equator spins once in about twenty-five days, but near the poles a turn takes about thirty-five. This uneven spin winds up the Sun's magnetic field like a spring, and that is what drives the sunspots, the flares and the whole eleven-year cycle.
- **sun-granulation** — Up close, the Sun's surface is boiling. Each bright cell is a column of hot gas the size of a country, rising from below, cooling, and sinking again at its dark edges. A single cell lasts only about ten minutes, and there are around four million of them on the Sun at any moment.
- **sun-neutrinos** — The Sun's core makes more than light. It also fires out neutrinos, ghostly particles that barely touch anything. Every second, tens of billions of them pass through your thumbnail, and straight through the whole Earth, day and night. Catching even a few of them takes a giant tank deep underground.
- **sun-red-giant** — The Sun will not last forever. In about five billion years, it will run short of hydrogen in its core and swell into a red giant, swallowing Mercury and Venus, and maybe Earth. Then it will puff off its outer layers as a glowing cloud, and shrink into a white dwarf, about the size of the Earth.
### Part 3L-2

- **lunar-eclipse** — In a total lunar eclipse, the Moon slides into Earth's shadow. But it does not disappear. It turns a deep coppery red, because Earth's atmosphere bends a little sunlight into the shadow. You are seeing the light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth, all at once, falling on the Moon.
- **annular-eclipse** — The Moon's orbit is an oval, so sometimes it is a little farther from us, and looks a little smaller. If an eclipse happens then, the Moon cannot cover the whole Sun. It leaves a blazing ring of fire around its edge. That is an annular eclipse, from the Latin word for a ring.
- **eclipse-2027** — On the second of August twenty twenty-seven, the Moon's shadow will sweep across North Africa and the Middle East. Over Luxor, in Egypt, the Sun will be completely hidden for more than six minutes, one of the longest total eclipses of the century. The Moon will be near its closest to Earth, and Earth near its farthest from the Sun.
- **supermoon** — The Moon's distance from us changes over every orbit. A full Moon near its closest point is called a supermoon. It looks about fourteen percent bigger and thirty percent brighter than a full Moon at its farthest. Side by side, the difference is clear, even though by eye it is hard to tell.
- **moon-phases** — The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. What changes is how much of that lit half faces us. As the Moon circles Earth each month, we see a thin crescent, then a half, then a full Moon, and back again. The phases are just the Moon's day and night, seen from here.
- **tides** — The Moon and the Sun both pull on Earth's oceans, raising two bulges of water on opposite sides of the planet. As Earth turns through them, the tide comes in and goes out about twice a day. When the Sun and Moon line up, we get the highest tides of all, spring tides. When they pull at right angles, the tides are gentle, neap tides.
- **tidal-locking** — The Moon always shows us the same face. That is not a coincidence. Long ago, Earth's pull on the Moon slowed its spin, until it turned exactly once for every trip around us. No one saw the far side until a spacecraft photographed it in nineteen fifty-nine. Most of the big moons in the Solar System are locked the same way.
- **libration** — The Moon does not hold perfectly still. Its oval orbit, and its slight tilt, make it seem to rock from side to side and nod up and down over a month. Thanks to that wobble, we can see a little way around its edges, about fifty-nine percent of its surface in all, not just half.
- **earthshine** — Look at a thin crescent Moon on a clear evening, and you can often see the rest of it, glowing faintly. That glow is earthshine. It is sunlight that has bounced off the Earth, travelled to the Moon, and bounced back to your eyes. Leonardo da Vinci was the first to explain it, five hundred years ago.
- **theia-impact** — Our Moon was probably born in a collision. About four and a half billion years ago, a world about the size of Mars, often called Theia, smashed into the young Earth. The impact threw a ring of molten rock into orbit, and within a short time, it gathered itself into the Moon.
- **moonquakes** — The Moon still quakes. Instruments left by the Apollo astronauts recorded thousands of moonquakes. Most are tiny, but shallow ones can reach magnitude five. And because the Moon is so dry and cold, it does not damp the shaking. The whole Moon can ring like a bell for many minutes.
- **chicxulub** — Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about ten kilometres wide struck what is now the coast of Mexico. It blasted a crater a hundred and eighty kilometres across, and threw so much dust and soot into the sky that the Sun was dimmed for years. Three quarters of all species died out, including every dinosaur that could not fly.
- **tunguska** — On the thirtieth of June nineteen-oh-eight, a space rock exploded in the air above Siberia. The blast flattened about eighty million trees, across more than two thousand square kilometres of forest. But it left no crater, because it burst a few kilometres up, before it ever reached the ground.
- **chelyabinsk** — On the fifteenth of February twenty thirteen, a rock about twenty metres wide streaked across the morning sky over Russia, briefly brighter than the Sun. It burst about thirty kilometres up. The shock wave arrived minutes later, shattering windows across the city of Chelyabinsk. No telescope had seen it coming.
- **daylight-fireball-1972** — On the tenth of August nineteen seventy-two, a space rock several metres across skimmed through Earth's upper atmosphere over the western United States and Canada. Hundreds of people saw it blaze across the daytime sky. Then, like a stone skipping on water, it bounced back out into space, and carried on around the Sun.
- **apophis-2029** — On Friday the thirteenth of April twenty twenty-nine, the asteroid Apophis, about three hundred and forty metres wide, will pass about thirty-two thousand kilometres from Earth's surface. That is closer than some satellites. It will be bright enough to see without a telescope, and we are now sure it will miss.
- **mini-moon-2024** — In the autumn of twenty twenty-four, Earth briefly had a second moon. A small asteroid, only about ten metres across, drifted slowly enough to be caught by Earth's gravity in a loose loop. It stayed for about two months, then drifted away again, back into orbit around the Sun.
- **leonid-storm-1833** — On the night of the twelfth of November eighteen thirty-three, the sky over North America filled with shooting stars, tens of thousands an hour, falling like snow. Earth had ploughed through a dense trail of dust left by Comet Tempel–Tuttle. The study of meteors as a science began that night.
- **precession** — Earth's axis wobbles, like a spinning top that is slowing down, tracing a slow circle in the sky once every twenty-six thousand years. Today our axis points at Polaris, the North Star. In about twelve thousand years, it will point near Vega, and Vega will be our pole star.
- **magnetic-reversal** — Earth's magnetic field is made by molten iron churning in the core, and every so often it flips. North becomes south. We can read the record in old lava flows, which froze with the field of their time locked inside. The last full reversal was about seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago.
- **steve** — STEVE is a narrow ribbon of purple light that sometimes appears in the sky, below the aurora. It was named by aurora watchers, and later given a scientific name to fit. It is not an aurora at all. It glows from a river of very hot gas, rushing westward high above the ground.
- **zodiacal-light** — On dark, clear nights, just after sunset or before dawn, a faint cone of light rises from the horizon along the path of the planets. It is zodiacal light: sunlight, scattered by countless grains of dust drifting between the planets, much of it shed by comets and asteroids.
### Part 3L-3

- **transit-mercury** — Now and then, Mercury passes directly between the Earth and the Sun. Through a safe solar telescope, it looks like a tiny black dot, crossing the Sun's face over a few hours. It happens about thirteen times a century. The last was in November twenty nineteen, and the next is in November twenty thirty-two.
- **transit-venus** — In June twenty twelve, Venus crossed the face of the Sun, a round black silhouette. Transits of Venus come in pairs, eight years apart, more than a century apart. In the past, astronomers sailed around the world to time them, and used them to measure the size of the Solar System. The next will not happen until December twenty-one seventeen.
- **mercury-double-sunrise** — On parts of Mercury, the Sun rises, stops, slips back below the horizon, and then rises again for good. Mercury spins slowly, and when it swings closest to the Sun, its orbit briefly runs faster than its spin, so the Sun seems to go backwards across the sky.
- **venus-superrotation** — Venus turns once every two hundred and forty-three days. But the top of its clouds races around the whole planet in just four days, sixty times faster than the ground below. Planetary scientists call it super-rotation, and exactly what drives it is still being worked out.
- **venus-volcano** — Venus is still volcanically alive. In twenty twenty-three, scientists looked again at radar images taken in nineteen ninety-one, eight months apart. They found a volcanic vent that had grown and changed shape in between, and filled with fresh lava. It was the first direct sign of a recent eruption on Venus.
- **mars-opposition** — About every two years and two months, Earth catches up with Mars on its faster inside orbit, and passes it. Mars then sits opposite the Sun in our sky, rising at sunset and shining all night at its brightest. It is the best time to see it, and the best time to launch spacecraft to it.
- **mars-retrograde** — Every two years or so, Mars seems to stop, move backwards across the stars for a few weeks, and then turn forward again. For thousands of years this loop puzzled sky watchers. It is only an illusion: Earth, on the faster inside track, is overtaking Mars, just as a car you pass on a motorway seems to slide backwards.
- **mars-blue-sunset** — On Mars, sunsets are blue. The fine red dust in its air scatters red light across the whole sky by day, but near the setting Sun, the dust scatters blue light forward, toward your eyes. So the glow around the Sun at dusk turns a cool, pale blue.
- **mars-dust-devils** — Whirlwinds sweep across Mars, much bigger than any on Earth, some of them several kilometres tall. As they wander across the plains, they lift away the bright surface dust and leave dark, looping tracks behind them, like scribbles drawn across the planet.
- **mars-spiders** — Every spring at Mars's south pole, the Sun shines through a layer of clear carbon dioxide ice and warms the ground underneath. The ice turns to gas from below, pressure builds, and it bursts out in jets of gas and dust. Year after year, they carve channels that look like giant spiders.
- **mars-avalanches** — In spring on Mars, near the north pole, the Sun warms cliffs of ice and dust half a kilometre tall. Frost cracks and breaks away, and avalanches crash down the slopes, throwing clouds of dust into the air. Spacecraft orbiting Mars have caught them in the act.
- **phobos-fall** — Mars's little moon Phobos orbits so close that it goes round faster than Mars spins, and the tides it raises are pulling it slowly inward, about two metres every hundred years. In thirty to fifty million years, Mars's gravity will tear it apart, and spread it into a ring around the planet.
- **asteroid-collision** — In January twenty-ten, astronomers found what looked like a comet in the asteroid belt, with a strange X-shaped head and a long dusty tail. It was not a comet. It was the wreckage of two asteroids that had smashed into each other, about a year before, at around five kilometres a second.
- **asteroid-spin-apart** — Sunlight can push on a small asteroid. Warmed on one side, it gives off heat unevenly, and over millions of years that tiny push can spin it faster and faster, until it can no longer hold together. In twenty thirteen, the Hubble telescope watched one asteroid break apart into ten pieces.
- **vesta-rheasilvia** — About a billion years ago, the asteroid Vesta was struck so hard that the impact gouged out a crater five hundred kilometres wide, almost as wide as Vesta itself. Its central mountain is more than twice as tall as Everest. Pieces blasted off Vesta still land on Earth today as meteorites.
- **ceres-bright-spots** — Ceres, the largest object in the asteroid belt, has bright spots that shine out of the dark crater Occator. They are salt. Salty water seeped up from below the surface, the water boiled away into space, and the salt was left behind, gleaming in the sunlight.
- **phaethon** — Phaethon is a rock that swoops in closer to the Sun than Mercury ever goes, baked to more than seven hundred degrees. The heat cracks it and sheds dust along its orbit. And every December, Earth passes through that dust, and we see the Geminid meteor shower, one of the best of the year.
- **bennu-particles** — When a spacecraft arrived at the asteroid Bennu, it saw something no one expected: Bennu was throwing pebbles into space. Small bits of rock pop off its surface every few days. Some escape, some go into orbit for a while, and some fall back down onto the asteroid.
- **jupiter-grs-shrinking** — Jupiter's Great Red Spot has been watched for nearly two hundred years, and it is getting smaller. In the eighteen-eighties, it was about forty thousand kilometres across, three Earths wide. Today it is about sixteen thousand, and it has become rounder. No one knows if it will one day vanish.
- **jupiter-belt-fade** — In twenty-ten, astronomers pointed their telescopes at Jupiter and found one of its two great dark belts was simply gone. The South Equatorial Belt had been hidden by a layer of bright, high clouds. Months later, a dark outbreak began, and the belt came back. It has faded and returned like this several times.
- **io-flux-tube** — Io and Jupiter are wired together. As Io moves through Jupiter's magnetic field, it acts like a giant generator, driving an electric current of millions of amps along the field lines between them. Where that current reaches Jupiter, it makes a glowing spot in the aurora, Io's own footprint.
- **laplace-resonance** — Three of Jupiter's big moons move in step. For every one orbit Ganymede makes, Europa makes two, and Io makes four. Their regular tugs on each other keep their orbits stretched, which keeps Io's volcanoes and Europa's ocean heated. And all three can never line up on the same side of Jupiter.
### Part 3L-4

- **jupiter-shadow-transit** — When one of Jupiter's big moons passes between Jupiter and the Sun, its shadow falls on the cloud tops as a small, perfectly black dot. Anyone standing under it on Jupiter would see a total eclipse. Sometimes two, or even three, moon shadows cross Jupiter at the same time.
- **europa-tides** — Europa is covered in ice, but underneath lies an ocean of salty water, with perhaps twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans. Jupiter's gravity squeezes and stretches Europa as it orbits, and that flexing warms the inside, keeping the ocean from freezing. It is one of the best places to look for life beyond Earth.
- **jupiter-lightning** — Jupiter has lightning, deep down in its water clouds, flashing in giant thunderstorms. The Juno spacecraft found it is most common near the poles. High above the storms, there are also brief red flashes called sprites, the same kind of upper-air lightning seen above thunderstorms on Earth.
- **saturn-hexagon** — At Saturn's north pole there is a jet stream shaped like a hexagon, with six straight sides, each one wider than the Earth. It has been there at least since the Voyager probes flew past in the early nineteen-eighties. It is thought to form where winds of different speeds meet, in a stable, wavy pattern.
- **ring-plane-crossing** — Saturn's rings are hundreds of thousands of kilometres wide, but in most places only about ten metres thick. So about every fifteen years, when Earth lines up with the plane of the rings, we see them edge-on, and they almost disappear. It last happened in March twenty twenty-five.
- **ring-rain** — Saturn's rings are slowly raining into the planet. Tiny ice grains become electrically charged and are pulled along the magnetic field, down into Saturn's atmosphere. The Cassini spacecraft measured this ring rain. At this rate, the rings may be mostly gone in a few hundred million years, so we may be living at the best time to see them.
- **ring-propellers** — Hidden in Saturn's rings are tiny moonlets, too small to see, only a few hundred metres across. As they orbit, their gravity stirs up the ring particles on either side, carving gaps shaped like aeroplane propellers. The Cassini spacecraft found many of them.
- **shepherd-moons** — Saturn's thin F ring is guarded by two small moons, Prometheus just inside it and Pandora just outside. Their gravity herds the ring's particles and keeps the ring narrow. And every time Prometheus swings close, it pulls out streamers of ice, twisting the ring into knots and braids.
- **titan-methane-rain** — Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has weather. It is far too cold for water to flow, but methane, which is a gas on Earth, falls there as rain. It carves rivers, and fills lakes and seas near the poles, under a thick orange haze. Titan is the only place besides Earth with liquid on its surface.
- **hyperion-tumble** — Saturn's moon Hyperion looks like a giant sponge, full of deep craters, because it is mostly empty space inside. And it does not spin in any regular way. Tugged by Saturn's big moon Titan, it tumbles chaotically, so its spin can never be predicted for long.
- **iapetus-two-faces** — Iapetus is a moon with two faces. The side that leads as it orbits is as dark as coal. The other side is as bright as snow. Dark dust swept up from farther out darkens the leading side, which then warms in the sunlight, and its ice moves away to the bright side, making the difference even starker.
- **uranus-tipped** — Uranus spins on its side. Its axis is tipped over by ninety-eight degrees, so it rolls around the Sun like a ball. The most likely explanation is a giant collision long ago, with a world several times the size of Earth, that knocked the young planet over.
- **uranus-seasons** — Because Uranus lies on its side, its seasons are extreme. During each eighty-four-year orbit, one pole points almost straight at the Sun for decades, while the other is in darkness. So each pole gets about forty-two years of sunlight, then forty-two years of night.
- **neptune-dark-spot** — In nineteen eighty-nine, the Voyager Two spacecraft found a dark storm on Neptune as big as the Earth, which became known as the Great Dark Spot. When the Hubble Space Telescope looked in nineteen ninety-four, it was gone. Neptune's storms come and go within a few years.
- **neptune-winds** — Neptune has the fastest winds in the Solar System, about two thousand kilometres an hour, faster than the speed of sound on Earth. And it is the planet farthest from the Sun, getting the least sunlight of all. Where the energy for such winds comes from is still a puzzle.
- **diamond-rain** — Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, the pressure is millions of times greater than on Earth. There, carbon from methane may be squeezed into diamonds, which slowly sink toward the core, like hail. Experiments with powerful lasers on Earth have made tiny diamonds in the same way.
- **triton-doom** — Triton, Neptune's largest moon, orbits backwards, against Neptune's spin. That means it was probably captured from the Kuiper Belt, long ago. Neptune's tides are slowly drawing it inward, and in a few billion years, it will be torn apart, and may become a ring.
- **pluto-charon-dance** — Pluto and its moon Charon are so close in size that they are almost a double world. They are locked face to face, each always showing the other the same side. And they both circle a point in the empty space between them, so Pluto wobbles as Charon goes round.
- **charon-red-cap** — Charon, Pluto's largest moon, has a red cap on its north pole. Gas slowly escapes from Pluto's atmosphere, and some of it is caught by Charon. In the long polar winter it freezes onto the pole, and when sunlight returns, it slowly turns the ice reddish brown.
- **arrokoth-merger** — Arrokoth, far beyond Pluto, is made of two small worlds stuck together, like a cosmic snowman. Billions of years ago, they circled each other, slowly drew together, and touched at walking pace, so gently that neither was broken. It shows how the very first building blocks of planets came together.
- **gliese-710** — In about one point three million years, a star called Gliese seven-ten will pass through the outer edge of our Solar System, about ten thousand times farther away than the Sun is from Earth. Its gravity will disturb the Oort Cloud, and send a shower of comets drifting inward over the following million years.
- **halley-2061** — Halley's Comet comes back about every seventy-six years. It was seen by ancient astronomers, and was woven into the Bayeux Tapestry in ten sixty-six. Edmond Halley predicted its return, and it came back, as he said, after his death. It will next be at its closest to the Sun in the summer of twenty sixty-one.
### Part 3L-5

- **hale-bopp** — In nineteen ninety-seven, Comet Hale–Bopp hung in our skies for about a year and a half, visible even from bright cities. Its icy core was about sixty kilometres wide, many times larger than most comets. It had two tails, a straight blue tail of gas and a broad white tail of dust.
- **tsuchinshan-atlas** — In October twenty twenty-four, Comet Tsuchinshan–ATLAS lit up evening skies around the world. For a few days it showed something rare: a thin spike pointing toward the Sun, an anti-tail. It was not really pointing sunward. We were seeing its dust trail from the side, lined up with Earth.
- **neowise** — In July twenty twenty, Comet NEOWISE swung past the Sun and became the brightest comet seen from the northern hemisphere in more than twenty years, with a long curving tail. It will not be back for about seven thousand years.
- **holmes-outburst** — In October twenty-oh-seven, Comet Holmes suddenly brightened about half a million times in less than a day, becoming visible without a telescope. Its expanding cloud of dust kept growing, until for a while, it was bigger than the Sun.
- **comet-breakup** — Comets can fall apart. Comet Schwassmann–Wachmann Three began breaking up in nineteen ninety-five. By two thousand and six, it had split into dozens of pieces, strung out along its orbit, each one with a tail of its own.
- **encke-tail** — In April twenty-oh-seven, a spacecraft was watching Comet Encke when a blast of plasma from the Sun swept over it. The comet's ion tail was snapped clean off, and drifted away into space. The comet soon grew a new one.
- **comet-two-tails** — A comet has two tails. One is made of dust, pushed gently by sunlight, so it curves along the comet's path and shines white. The other is made of gas, blown straight outward by the solar wind, glowing blue. Both always point away from the Sun, whichever way the comet is moving.
- **great-conjunction** — Every twenty years, Jupiter and Saturn appear to pass each other in our sky. In December twenty twenty they came within a tenth of a degree, closer than they had looked since sixteen twenty-three. Through a small telescope, both planets and their moons fitted in the same view.
- **planet-parade** — Sometimes several planets gather along one arc of the evening sky, and people call it a planet parade. But they are not lined up in space. All the planets orbit in nearly the same flat plane, so from Earth we always see them along the same path across the sky.
- **lunar-occultation** — Now and then, the Moon slides in front of a planet and hides it. Because the Moon has no air, the planet does not fade. It vanishes at the Moon's edge in a second or two. When Saturn is hidden, you can watch its rings disappear, and then reappear on the other side.
- **betelgeuse-dimming** — In late twenty nineteen, Betelgeuse, the red giant star at Orion's shoulder, faded to about a third of its usual brightness. Some wondered if it was about to explode. It was not. It had puffed out a cloud of gas that cooled into dust and blocked part of its light. By the spring, it was back.
- **t-crb-nova** — T Coronae Borealis is a star we are waiting for. It is a pair of stars, and every eighty years or so, the small, dense one ignites the gas it has stolen from its companion, in a nuclear explosion on its surface. It last erupted in nineteen forty-six. When it does again, it will briefly shine as bright as the North Star.
- **grb-boat** — On the ninth of October twenty twenty-two, the brightest gamma-ray burst ever recorded reached Earth. It came from a giant star collapsing into a black hole, about two billion light-years away. It was so powerful that, even from that distance, it disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere. Astronomers nicknamed it the BOAT: the brightest of all time.
- **sn-1987a** — In February nineteen eighty-seven, a star exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy near our own. It was the closest supernova seen in nearly four hundred years, visible without a telescope from the southern hemisphere. And hours before the light arrived, detectors on Earth caught a burst of neutrinos from the star's collapsing core.
- **crab-supernova** — In the year ten fifty-four, astronomers in China recorded a new star, so bright it could be seen in daylight for more than three weeks. It was a supernova. Today, in the same spot, we see the Crab Nebula, still expanding, with a dead star at its centre, spinning thirty times every second.
- **gw150914** — On the fourteenth of September twenty fifteen, detectors on Earth felt a tiny shiver in space itself. Two black holes, about thirty times the mass of the Sun each, had spiralled together and merged, one point three billion years ago. It was the first gravitational wave ever detected, a century after Einstein predicted them.
- **magnetar-flare** — On the twenty-seventh of December twenty-oh-four, a magnetar, a dead star with the strongest magnetic field known, let out a giant flare. In a fraction of a second, it released more energy than the Sun gives off in a hundred thousand years. Though it was about fifty thousand light-years away, it disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere.
- **tidal-disruption** — When a star strays too close to a giant black hole, the black hole's gravity pulls harder on its near side than its far side. The star is stretched out into a long stream of gas, spaghettified. About half of it is flung away, and the rest falls in, flaring brightly for months.
- **sgr-a-flares** — At the heart of our galaxy is a black hole four million times the mass of the Sun, called Sagittarius A star. Most of the time it is quiet. But about once a day, it flickers and flares, as clumps of hot gas swirl close to its edge.
- **fast-radio-bursts** — Fast radio bursts are flashes of radio waves lasting just a thousandth of a second, coming from other galaxies. In that instant, each one releases as much energy as the Sun does in days. Some repeat. At least one has been traced to a magnetar inside our own galaxy.
- **proxima-superflare** — Proxima Centauri, the closest star to the Sun, is a small red dwarf with a violent temper. In March twenty sixteen, it flared to about seventy times its normal brightness, for a few minutes. Flares like that would scorch any planet orbiting close by, including the rocky planet we know is there.
- **milky-way-andromeda** — The Andromeda galaxy, two and a half million light-years away, is heading toward us. For years we thought the two galaxies would collide in about four and a half billion years. A newer study in twenty twenty-five puts the chance of a merger in the next ten billion years at about one half. Even then, the stars themselves would almost never touch.
