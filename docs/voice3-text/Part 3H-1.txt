An A.U. — an astronomical unit — is the distance from Earth to the Sun: about a hundred and fifty million kilometres. It is the ruler we use for the Solar System. Light crosses it in just over eight minutes, and Neptune is thirty of them away.

Light travels almost three hundred thousand kilometres every second — the fastest anything can go. Fast enough to circle Earth seven and a half times in one second. And still, space is so big that light has to wait.

Light takes time to reach us, so every view is a look into the past. You see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago, and Neptune as it was four hours ago. The further you look, the older the light.

A light-year is a distance, not a time: how far light travels in one year — about nine and a half trillion kilometres. The nearest star to the Sun is just over four light-years away.

A parsec is about three and a quarter light-years. As Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift very slightly against the stars far behind it. Measuring that tiny shift is how we first found the distances to the stars.

The inner planets are the four small, rocky worlds closest to the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. They have solid ground, thin air or none at all, and hardly any moons.

The main asteroid belt is a wide ring of rocky leftovers between Mars and Jupiter. There are millions of rocks — but spread so thinly that spacecraft fly straight through without having to dodge. Jupiter's pull stopped them from ever gathering into a planet.

The giant planets — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune — are huge worlds of gas and ice, with no solid ground to stand on. Every one of them has rings, and dozens of moons.

The Kuiper Belt is a thick ring of icy worlds beyond Neptune. It is like the asteroid belt — but far wider, and made mostly of ice. Pluto lives here, along with thousands of others.

The Plutinos go round the Sun twice for every three laps of Neptune, in step with Pluto — which is where their name comes from. That rhythm means Neptune is never nearby when they cross its path, so they are never thrown out.

The classical Kuiper Belt is the belt's main crowd, on calm, nearly round orbits that Neptune never disturbed. It is so peaceful out here that many of its worlds are still in pairs, just as they formed.

The scattered disc is made of worlds that Neptune kicked, long ago, onto long, stretched, tilted orbits. They swing in close to Neptune, then out to hundreds of times Earth's distance from the Sun.
