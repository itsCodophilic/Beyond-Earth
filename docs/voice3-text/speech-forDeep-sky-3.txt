Could there be shortcuts through space? Einstein's theory says that mass curves space. Drawn as a sheet, a heavy object makes a funnel. In nineteen thirty-five, Albert Einstein and Nathan Rosen found that the equations allow two such funnels joined at the throat: a bridge between two distant places, now called a wormhole. From outside, its mouth would look like a sphere, showing the sky at the far end. But such a bridge would pinch shut faster than light could cross it. To hold one open would need exotic matter with negative energy, which ordinary matter does not have, and which may not exist in large amounts. No wormhole has ever been found. It is a beautiful solution of the equations, and no one yet knows whether nature uses it.

In the constellation of Canes Venatici is a faint blue dot of light that is not a star. It is TON six eighteen, a quasar, seen as it was ten point eight billion years ago, before the Sun and Earth existed. At its centre is one of the heaviest black holes known, about forty billion times the mass of the Sun. Gas swirling into it is so hot that it outshines its whole galaxy. Its event horizon, the edge from which nothing escapes, would be about eight hundred times the distance from the Earth to the Sun. Our entire planetary system, out to Neptune, is a speck beside it. Even the Voyager spacecraft, after nearly fifty years of travel, would still be deep inside.

A black hole is a place nothing can leave. Einstein's equations work just as well backwards in time, and run backwards, a black hole becomes a white hole: a place nothing can enter, that only pours out matter and light. Gas falling toward it would be turned back before it arrived. In twenty twelve, two astronomers suggested that a strange gamma-ray burst, GRB oh six oh six one four, seen in June two thousand and six from a galaxy one point six billion light-years away, might have been one. It lasted a hundred and two seconds, but no supernova followed, which bursts that long usually have. Most astronomers think it was something else. No white hole has ever been confirmed, and nothing we know of would make one.

How big can a star be? Among the largest known is Stephenson two dash eighteen, a red supergiant about nineteen thousand light-years away, in a star cluster in Scutum, hidden from our eyes by dust. Its width has been estimated at about two thousand one hundred and fifty times the Sun's. Put where the Sun is, its surface would reach out past the orbit of Saturn. Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn would all be inside it. Uranus and Neptune would still be outside, so it would not quite swallow the whole Solar System, and its true size is uncertain; it may be smaller. Stars like this are near the end of their lives, their outer layers ballooned out, so thin that they are emptier than the air around you.

In nineteen sixty, the physicist Freeman Dyson suggested that a very advanced civilisation might surround its star with a swarm of collectors to catch all of its energy. We could look for them, he said, by the starlight they block, and the heat they give off. In twenty fifteen, volunteers looking through data from the Kepler telescope found a star, about fourteen hundred and seventy light-years away in Cygnus, that dimmed in a way no one could explain: by fifteen percent, then by twenty-two percent, irregularly. It became known as Tabby's star, after the astronomer Tabetha Boyajian. Could it be a Dyson swarm? In twenty eighteen, astronomers found that the dips were deeper in blue light than in red. Solid machines would block every colour equally. Fine dust does not. It was dust. No Dyson sphere has been found, but astronomers still look.

Galaxies are not scattered evenly through space. Over billions of years, gravity has gathered them into walls and threads, around enormous empty bubbles, like the foam of a sponge. Beyond the stars of Boötes, about seven hundred million light-years away, is one of the biggest bubbles known: the Boötes Void, about three hundred and thirty million light-years across. Where there would normally be thousands of galaxies, astronomers have found only about sixty. If our Milky Way sat in the middle of it, we might not have known that other galaxies existed until the nineteen sixties.

In November nineteen sixty-seven, a young astronomer at Cambridge, Jocelyn Bell Burnell, noticed a strange signal on her radio telescope's chart. Pulses, exactly one point three three seven seconds apart, as steady as a clock. Half jokingly, it was labelled LGM one, for little green men. It was the first pulsar, now called PSR B nineteen nineteen plus twenty-one, about two thousand three hundred light-years away. A pulsar is a neutron star, the collapsed core of a dead star, twenty kilometres across, spinning fast. Beams of radio waves pour out above its magnetic poles, and as it spins, they sweep around like a lighthouse. Each time a beam crosses Earth, we hear a pulse. More than three thousand pulsars are now known, and some spin more than seven hundred times a second.

In April twenty fourteen, astronomers announced the first planet found that is about the size of Earth, and at the right distance from its star for liquid water. It is called Kepler one eighty-six f. Its star is a red dwarf, about half the Sun's mass, about five hundred and eighty light-years away in Cygnus. It has five planets. Four orbit very close in, and are too hot. The fifth, f, is just over one Earth wide, and goes round every hundred and thirty days, at the cool edge of the habitable zone, where water could stay liquid. It receives about a third of the sunlight Earth does. Whether it has air, or oceans, or life, no one yet knows. Since then, more such worlds have been found, around Proxima Centauri, and the star TRAPPIST one.
