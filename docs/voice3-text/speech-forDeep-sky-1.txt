What is a nova? The word is Latin for new. For thousands of years, people now and then saw a new star appear where there had been none, blaze for a while, and fade. We now know that most of these are not new stars at all, but old ones, flaring up. And there are very different ways for a star to flare.

A true nova happens on a white dwarf, the small, dense, Earth-sized core left behind when a star like the Sun dies. If a white dwarf has a partner star close by, its gravity can pull gas off that partner. The gas piles up on the white dwarf's surface, squeezed and heated, until it ignites all at once, like a hydrogen bomb the size of a planet. The layer is blown off into space, the star shines thousands of times brighter for a few weeks, and then it settles down. The white dwarf survives, and starts collecting again.

A supernova is something far bigger. Super means it destroys the whole star. Either a white dwarf grows too heavy and blows itself completely apart, or a giant star many times the Sun's mass runs out of fuel, and its core collapses in less than a second. For a few weeks, one supernova can outshine an entire galaxy of a hundred billion stars.

And a kilonova is the flash when two neutron stars, the crushed cores of dead giant stars, crash into each other. It is about a thousand times brighter than a nova, which is where its name comes from, but much fainter than a supernova. Its debris forges gold, platinum and uranium. So, a nova is a flare on a surviving star. A supernova is the death of a star. And a kilonova is the collision of two dead ones. Let us travel to each of them.

In August nineteen seventy-five, a new star appeared in the constellation of Cygnus, the Swan. One night there was nothing there that any ordinary telescope could see. By the next evening it was one of the brightest stars in the sky. Its name is V fifteen hundred Cygni, and it lies about five thousand one hundred light-years away. There, a white dwarf circles a small red dwarf star every three hours and twenty-one minutes, so close that the white dwarf steals its gas. The stolen hydrogen spirals down in a disc and settles on the white dwarf's surface. When the layer reached about twenty million degrees, it ignited in a thermonuclear runaway. In about a day, the star became more than forty million times brighter: the fastest nova ever recorded. Then the white dwarf, still there, began collecting again. This is a classical nova. Astronomers find several of them every year in our galaxy.

Some novae do not wait thousands of years between eruptions. RS Ophiuchi, about five thousand light-years away in Ophiuchus, has erupted at least nine times since eighteen ninety-eight: in nineteen thirty-three, fifty-eight, sixty-seven, eighty-five, two thousand and six, and most recently in August twenty twenty-one. Here the white dwarf circles a red giant, and drinks from the giant's wind. This white dwarf is very heavy, close to the most a white dwarf can be, so its gravity crushes the stolen gas hard, and only a thin layer is needed to set it off. That is why it erupts every fifteen to twenty years. Each blast slams into the red giant's wind, making shock waves that glow in X-rays, and even accelerate particles to enormous energies. From Earth, RS Ophiuchi jumps from far too faint to see to just visible to the naked eye, for a few days. This is a recurrent nova.

In twenty twenty-two, astronomers using NASA's TESS satellite found something new: tiny novae. On a white dwarf called TV Columbae, about sixteen hundred light-years away, they saw bursts lasting only about ten hours. This white dwarf has a magnetic field strong enough to grab the gas from its partner before it can form a disc, and channel it down onto the two magnetic poles. There, the hydrogen collects in a small patch. When the patch ignites, only that patch burns. Each burst still consumes about twenty million billion kilograms of material, about three and a half billion Great Pyramids of Giza, yet it is only a millionth the size of an ordinary nova. Astronomers called them micronovae.

Some novae are in no hurry at all. In nineteen forty-four, a red giant star in the southern constellation of Telescopium, called RR Telescopii, began to brighten. It kept on brightening for four years. And then, instead of fading in a few weeks like most novae, it stayed bright for decades. Eighty years later, it is still fading. RR Telescopii is a symbiotic star: a pulsating red giant, shedding gas in a slow wind, and a white dwarf, far out from it, catching a little of that wind. Because this white dwarf is light, its gas must pile very deep before it ignites, and then it burns steadily, for a lifetime, rather than blowing away in days. It lies about eight thousand eight hundred light-years away. This is a symbiotic nova, the slowest kind.

On the eleventh of November fifteen seventy-two, the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe saw a new star in Cassiopeia, brighter than Jupiter. It stayed visible for sixteen months. Tycho measured it carefully and showed it did not move against the stars, so it was far beyond the Moon, proving that the heavens could change. In two thousand and eight, astronomers caught its light echoing off distant dust, and finally took its spectrum. It was a Type One A supernova. About eight thousand light-years away, a white dwarf had been gaining mass from a companion. As it crept toward one point four times the mass of the Sun, the most a white dwarf can hold up, its carbon ignited everywhere at once. In about a second, the whole star was turned into new elements and flung into space at thousands of kilometres a second. Nothing at all was left behind. Because these explosions are always about equally bright, astronomers use them to measure the universe, and in nineteen ninety-eight they used them to discover that its expansion is speeding up.

Around the year sixteen seventy, the light of a supernova reached Earth from Cassiopeia, and almost no one noticed, because dust in the Milky Way dimmed it. Today we see what is left: Cassiopeia A, a shell of glowing debris about ten light-years across, eleven thousand light-years away, the youngest supernova remnant known in our galaxy. Its star was a giant of perhaps fifteen to twenty Suns. Giants like that burn hydrogen into helium, then carbon, oxygen, silicon, and finally iron. But iron cannot be burned for energy. With nothing to hold it up, the iron core collapsed in less than a second, into a ball of neutrons about twenty kilometres across. The falling layers bounced off it, and the star exploded. In nineteen ninety-nine, the Chandra X-ray telescope spotted the neutron star, still glowing at its centre. This is a core-collapse supernova, and every atom of iron in your blood was made in one.

On the seventeenth of August twenty seventeen, detectors in the United States and Italy felt space itself shiver for about a hundred seconds. One point seven seconds after the shiver ended, a satellite caught a burst of gamma rays from the same part of the sky. Within hours, telescopes found a new point of light in a galaxy called NGC forty-nine ninety-three, a hundred and thirty million light-years away. Two neutron stars, each heavier than the Sun but only about twenty kilometres wide, had spiralled together and collided. Their debris, flung out at a third of the speed of light, was so rich in neutrons that heavy elements formed in seconds: gold, platinum, uranium. It glowed blue for a day, then red for a week. This was a kilonova, watched by about seventy observatories, and it showed where much of the gold on Earth was made.

On the twenty-fifth of April nineteen ninety-eight, a satellite caught a gamma-ray burst. In the same spot, astronomers found a supernova, in a spiral galaxy about a hundred and twenty million light-years away. It was the first time a gamma-ray burst was tied to an exploding star. SN nineteen ninety-eight b w was a hypernova. A very massive star, stripped of its outer layers and spinning fast, collapsed straight into a black hole. Gas swirling into the black hole drove two jets out through the star at nearly the speed of light, and the star was blown apart with about ten times the energy of an ordinary supernova. A hypernova is about energy. There are also superluminous supernovae, about brightness, up to a hundred times brighter than usual, which may be powered in other ways. Both are rare: perhaps one in a hundred of the stars that explode.

In January two thousand and two, a faint star in Monoceros suddenly brightened. Over the next weeks it flared again and again, to about a million times the brightness of the Sun, and then did something no nova does: it turned deep red, and swelled into one of the largest stars in the galaxy. V eight thirty-eight Monocerotis, about nineteen thousand light-years away, is best explained as two stars that collided and merged. And its flash travelled outward through dust the stars had shed long before, lighting it up shell by shell. For years, the Hubble telescope watched this light echo spread, like ripples on a pond. In two thousand and eight, another star, V thirteen oh nine Scorpii, was caught before, during and after such a merger, proving that it happens. These are luminous red novae: not a flare on a white dwarf, but two ordinary stars becoming one.
