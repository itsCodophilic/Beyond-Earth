Now and then, Mercury passes directly between the Earth and the Sun. Through a safe solar telescope, it looks like a tiny black dot, crossing the Sun's face over a few hours. It happens about thirteen times a century. The last was in November twenty nineteen, and the next is in November twenty thirty-two.

In June twenty twelve, Venus crossed the face of the Sun, a round black silhouette. Transits of Venus come in pairs, eight years apart, more than a century apart. In the past, astronomers sailed around the world to time them, and used them to measure the size of the Solar System. The next will not happen until December twenty-one seventeen.

On parts of Mercury, the Sun rises, stops, slips back below the horizon, and then rises again for good. Mercury spins slowly, and when it swings closest to the Sun, its orbit briefly runs faster than its spin, so the Sun seems to go backwards across the sky.

Venus turns once every two hundred and forty-three days. But the top of its clouds races around the whole planet in just four days, sixty times faster than the ground below. Planetary scientists call it super-rotation, and exactly what drives it is still being worked out.

Venus is still volcanically alive. In twenty twenty-three, scientists looked again at radar images taken in nineteen ninety-one, eight months apart. They found a volcanic vent that had grown and changed shape in between, and filled with fresh lava. It was the first direct sign of a recent eruption on Venus.

About every two years and two months, Earth catches up with Mars on its faster inside orbit, and passes it. Mars then sits opposite the Sun in our sky, rising at sunset and shining all night at its brightest. It is the best time to see it, and the best time to launch spacecraft to it.

Every two years or so, Mars seems to stop, move backwards across the stars for a few weeks, and then turn forward again. For thousands of years this loop puzzled sky watchers. It is only an illusion: Earth, on the faster inside track, is overtaking Mars, just as a car you pass on a motorway seems to slide backwards.

On Mars, sunsets are blue. The fine red dust in its air scatters red light across the whole sky by day, but near the setting Sun, the dust scatters blue light forward, toward your eyes. So the glow around the Sun at dusk turns a cool, pale blue.

Whirlwinds sweep across Mars, much bigger than any on Earth, some of them several kilometres tall. As they wander across the plains, they lift away the bright surface dust and leave dark, looping tracks behind them, like scribbles drawn across the planet.

Every spring at Mars's south pole, the Sun shines through a layer of clear carbon dioxide ice and warms the ground underneath. The ice turns to gas from below, pressure builds, and it bursts out in jets of gas and dust. Year after year, they carve channels that look like giant spiders.

In spring on Mars, near the north pole, the Sun warms cliffs of ice and dust half a kilometre tall. Frost cracks and breaks away, and avalanches crash down the slopes, throwing clouds of dust into the air. Spacecraft orbiting Mars have caught them in the act.

Mars's little moon Phobos orbits so close that it goes round faster than Mars spins, and the tides it raises are pulling it slowly inward, about two metres every hundred years. In thirty to fifty million years, Mars's gravity will tear it apart, and spread it into a ring around the planet.

In January twenty-ten, astronomers found what looked like a comet in the asteroid belt, with a strange X-shaped head and a long dusty tail. It was not a comet. It was the wreckage of two asteroids that had smashed into each other, about a year before, at around five kilometres a second.

Sunlight can push on a small asteroid. Warmed on one side, it gives off heat unevenly, and over millions of years that tiny push can spin it faster and faster, until it can no longer hold together. In twenty thirteen, the Hubble telescope watched one asteroid break apart into ten pieces.

About a billion years ago, the asteroid Vesta was struck so hard that the impact gouged out a crater five hundred kilometres wide, almost as wide as Vesta itself. Its central mountain is more than twice as tall as Everest. Pieces blasted off Vesta still land on Earth today as meteorites.

Ceres, the largest object in the asteroid belt, has bright spots that shine out of the dark crater Occator. They are salt. Salty water seeped up from below the surface, the water boiled away into space, and the salt was left behind, gleaming in the sunlight.

Phaethon is a rock that swoops in closer to the Sun than Mercury ever goes, baked to more than seven hundred degrees. The heat cracks it and sheds dust along its orbit. And every December, Earth passes through that dust, and we see the Geminid meteor shower, one of the best of the year.

When a spacecraft arrived at the asteroid Bennu, it saw something no one expected: Bennu was throwing pebbles into space. Small bits of rock pop off its surface every few days. Some escape, some go into orbit for a while, and some fall back down onto the asteroid.

Jupiter's Great Red Spot has been watched for nearly two hundred years, and it is getting smaller. In the eighteen-eighties, it was about forty thousand kilometres across, three Earths wide. Today it is about sixteen thousand, and it has become rounder. No one knows if it will one day vanish.

In twenty-ten, astronomers pointed their telescopes at Jupiter and found one of its two great dark belts was simply gone. The South Equatorial Belt had been hidden by a layer of bright, high clouds. Months later, a dark outbreak began, and the belt came back. It has faded and returned like this several times.

Io and Jupiter are wired together. As Io moves through Jupiter's magnetic field, it acts like a giant generator, driving an electric current of millions of amps along the field lines between them. Where that current reaches Jupiter, it makes a glowing spot in the aurora, Io's own footprint.

Three of Jupiter's big moons move in step. For every one orbit Ganymede makes, Europa makes two, and Io makes four. Their regular tugs on each other keep their orbits stretched, which keeps Io's volcanoes and Europa's ocean heated. And all three can never line up on the same side of Jupiter.
