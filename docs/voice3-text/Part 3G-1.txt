This is Juno, the third asteroid ever found, back in eighteen oh four — and one of the largest in the whole belt.

This is Europa — not Jupiter's moon, but an asteroid with the same name. It is one of the biggest in the belt, dark and rich in carbon.

This is Davida, one of the largest asteroids of all — a dark, rounded world about three hundred kilometres across.

This is Eunomia, the biggest stony asteroid of all, and the parent of a whole family of smaller pieces that share its path.

This is Cybele, a large, dark asteroid out at the far edge of the main belt, where it gives its name to a little group of rocks.

This is Camilla, a big, dark asteroid beyond the main belt, with two small moons of its own.

This is Kalliope, a metal-rich asteroid with a large moon called Linus — named after her son, in Greek legend.

This is Linus, the moon of the asteroid Kalliope — nearly thirty kilometres across, which is big for an asteroid's moon.

This is Themis, a dark asteroid where astronomers found frost — water ice, right on the surface of an asteroid.

This is Flora, the parent of one of the largest asteroid families — and possibly the source of many of the meteorites that land on Earth.

This is Romulus, the larger of the two moons of the asteroid Sylvia — named after one of the twin founders of Rome.

This is Remus, the smaller of Sylvia's two moons — named after Romulus's twin brother.

This is Dactyl, the first moon ever found around an asteroid. It circles Ida, and it is barely a kilometre and a half across.

This is Dimorphos, the little moon that a spacecraft crashed into on purpose, in twenty twenty-two. The impact shortened its orbit by thirty-two minutes — proof that we can push a dangerous rock off course.

This is Dinkinesh, a tiny asteroid that the Lucy spacecraft flew past in twenty twenty-three. Lucy found it has a moon — and that moon is two rocks stuck together.

This is Selam, the moon of Dinkinesh — two little rocks resting against each other, the first moon of its kind ever found.

This is Donaldjohanson, named after the scientist who found Lucy, the famous fossil of an early human ancestor. The Lucy spacecraft flew past it in twenty twenty-five, and found a long, strange shape.

This is Comet Borrelly, shaped like a bowling pin. A spacecraft called Deep Space One photographed it up close in two thousand and one.
