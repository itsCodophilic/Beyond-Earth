In nineteen ninety-seven, Comet Hale–Bopp hung in our skies for about a year and a half, visible even from bright cities. Its icy core was about sixty kilometres wide, many times larger than most comets. It had two tails, a straight blue tail of gas and a broad white tail of dust.

In October twenty twenty-four, Comet Tsuchinshan–ATLAS lit up evening skies around the world. For a few days it showed something rare: a thin spike pointing toward the Sun, an anti-tail. It was not really pointing sunward. We were seeing its dust trail from the side, lined up with Earth.

In July twenty twenty, Comet NEOWISE swung past the Sun and became the brightest comet seen from the northern hemisphere in more than twenty years, with a long curving tail. It will not be back for about seven thousand years.

In October twenty-oh-seven, Comet Holmes suddenly brightened about half a million times in less than a day, becoming visible without a telescope. Its expanding cloud of dust kept growing, until for a while, it was bigger than the Sun.

Comets can fall apart. Comet Schwassmann–Wachmann Three began breaking up in nineteen ninety-five. By two thousand and six, it had split into dozens of pieces, strung out along its orbit, each one with a tail of its own.

In April twenty-oh-seven, a spacecraft was watching Comet Encke when a blast of plasma from the Sun swept over it. The comet's ion tail was snapped clean off, and drifted away into space. The comet soon grew a new one.

A comet has two tails. One is made of dust, pushed gently by sunlight, so it curves along the comet's path and shines white. The other is made of gas, blown straight outward by the solar wind, glowing blue. Both always point away from the Sun, whichever way the comet is moving.

Every twenty years, Jupiter and Saturn appear to pass each other in our sky. In December twenty twenty they came within a tenth of a degree, closer than they had looked since sixteen twenty-three. Through a small telescope, both planets and their moons fitted in the same view.

Sometimes several planets gather along one arc of the evening sky, and people call it a planet parade. But they are not lined up in space. All the planets orbit in nearly the same flat plane, so from Earth we always see them along the same path across the sky.

Now and then, the Moon slides in front of a planet and hides it. Because the Moon has no air, the planet does not fade. It vanishes at the Moon's edge in a second or two. When Saturn is hidden, you can watch its rings disappear, and then reappear on the other side.

In late twenty nineteen, Betelgeuse, the red giant star at Orion's shoulder, faded to about a third of its usual brightness. Some wondered if it was about to explode. It was not. It had puffed out a cloud of gas that cooled into dust and blocked part of its light. By the spring, it was back.

T Coronae Borealis is a star we are waiting for. It is a pair of stars, and every eighty years or so, the small, dense one ignites the gas it has stolen from its companion, in a nuclear explosion on its surface. It last erupted in nineteen forty-six. When it does again, it will briefly shine as bright as the North Star.

On the ninth of October twenty twenty-two, the brightest gamma-ray burst ever recorded reached Earth. It came from a giant star collapsing into a black hole, about two billion light-years away. It was so powerful that, even from that distance, it disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere. Astronomers nicknamed it the BOAT: the brightest of all time.

In February nineteen eighty-seven, a star exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy near our own. It was the closest supernova seen in nearly four hundred years, visible without a telescope from the southern hemisphere. And hours before the light arrived, detectors on Earth caught a burst of neutrinos from the star's collapsing core.

In the year ten fifty-four, astronomers in China recorded a new star, so bright it could be seen in daylight for more than three weeks. It was a supernova. Today, in the same spot, we see the Crab Nebula, still expanding, with a dead star at its centre, spinning thirty times every second.

On the fourteenth of September twenty fifteen, detectors on Earth felt a tiny shiver in space itself. Two black holes, about thirty times the mass of the Sun each, had spiralled together and merged, one point three billion years ago. It was the first gravitational wave ever detected, a century after Einstein predicted them.

On the twenty-seventh of December twenty-oh-four, a magnetar, a dead star with the strongest magnetic field known, let out a giant flare. In a fraction of a second, it released more energy than the Sun gives off in a hundred thousand years. Though it was about fifty thousand light-years away, it disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere.

When a star strays too close to a giant black hole, the black hole's gravity pulls harder on its near side than its far side. The star is stretched out into a long stream of gas, spaghettified. About half of it is flung away, and the rest falls in, flaring brightly for months.

At the heart of our galaxy is a black hole four million times the mass of the Sun, called Sagittarius A star. Most of the time it is quiet. But about once a day, it flickers and flares, as clumps of hot gas swirl close to its edge.

Fast radio bursts are flashes of radio waves lasting just a thousandth of a second, coming from other galaxies. In that instant, each one releases as much energy as the Sun does in days. Some repeat. At least one has been traced to a magnetar inside our own galaxy.

Proxima Centauri, the closest star to the Sun, is a small red dwarf with a violent temper. In March twenty sixteen, it flared to about seventy times its normal brightness, for a few minutes. Flares like that would scorch any planet orbiting close by, including the rocky planet we know is there.

The Andromeda galaxy, two and a half million light-years away, is heading toward us. For years we thought the two galaxies would collide in about four and a half billion years. A newer study in twenty twenty-five puts the chance of a merger in the next ten billion years at about one half. Even then, the stars themselves would almost never touch.
