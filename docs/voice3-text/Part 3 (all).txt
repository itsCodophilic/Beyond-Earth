Beneath the clouds of Venus lies a surface no eye has ever seen directly. Radar mapped it — volcanoes, lava plains and mountains, hidden under the thickest air of any rocky planet.

Jupiter does not travel alone. Look at the two great swarms of rocks on its path — one sixty degrees ahead of it, one sixty degrees behind. These are the Trojans, more than eleven thousand of them. At those two spots, the pull of the Sun and the pull of Jupiter balance just right, so a rock there keeps pace with the giant for ever. Jupiter is always heading for the ones in front, the ones behind are always following — and no one ever catches up. They have waited there for billions of years. Closer in are the Hildas. They go round the Sun three times for every two of Jupiter's laps, timed so that whenever Jupiter passes, they are far away. Seen from Jupiter, their paths trace a slowly turning triangle.

Why do we only ever see one face of the Moon? The Moon does spin — but it turns exactly once each time it goes around Earth, every twenty-seven days. So the same side always faces us. It was not always this way. Long ago, the young Moon spun faster. Earth's gravity stretched it slightly into an egg shape, and Earth's pull on that bulge acted like a brake, slowing its spin until the two rhythms matched. Then the braking stopped: the Moon was locked. Nearly every big moon in the Solar System has done the same. And the Moon is doing it to us, too — every century, our day grows about two thousandths of a second longer. No one saw the far side of the Moon until nineteen fifty-nine.

Between Jupiter and Neptune, there are no safe orbits. Yet more than a thousand icy worlds wander here. They are the Centaurs — named after the half-human, half-horse creatures of legend, because they are half asteroid, half comet. They began beyond Neptune, until Neptune nudged them inward. Now the giant planets pass them along like a ball — Uranus, Saturn, Jupiter. None of them stays long: a few million years, a blink in the life of the Solar System. Some are thrown out towards the stars. Others are pushed further in, warm up, and become comets. And they come in two colours — grey, or deep red. Nobody yet knows why.

Watch Jupiter's three big moons keep time. For every lap Ganymede makes around Jupiter, Europa makes exactly two, and Io makes exactly four. Because the rhythm repeats, the moons keep meeting at the same places, and every meeting gives the same small tug. The tugs add up, and keep their orbits slightly stretched. And strangely, all three can never line up together on the same side of Jupiter. This clockwork is what makes Io the most volcanic world we know. Jupiter squeezes it, then lets go, again and again, and its ground rises and falls by about a hundred metres. The same squeezing keeps Europa's hidden ocean from freezing.

Every planet has two calm spots on its path — one sixty degrees ahead of it, one sixty degrees behind — where a small rock can ride along at exactly the planet's pace. Venus has one known Trojan there. Earth has two. Mars has seventeen. But having the spots is not the same as keeping anything in them. Mars's Trojans have stayed for billions of years — and most of them are broken-off pieces of one rock, called Eureka. Earth's and Venus's are only visitors. They drift in, ride along with us for a while, and drift away again.

Out among the giants, the same trick works. Neptune has forty-two known Trojans, riding sixty degrees ahead of it and behind, and they have been there for billions of years. Uranus has just two, and they are only passing through. And Saturn? Not a single one has ever been found. Long ago, Jupiter's pull and the wandering of the young giant planets swept Saturn's calm spots clean.

The asteroid belt looks like a smooth ring of rocks. But count the asteroids by the size of their orbits, and it has holes in it. These are the Kirkwood gaps, first noticed in eighteen sixty-six. Each gap is a place where a rock would keep time with Jupiter — three laps for every one of Jupiter's, or five for every two. Meeting Jupiter at the same spot again and again, it gets pushed the same way every time, until it is flung out of the belt altogether. Some of those rocks are sent towards Earth — which is why so many of the meteorites that land here came from these gaps. And some asteroids belong to families: the broken pieces of one bigger rock, shattered in a collision, still travelling on almost the same path.

Out here, more than twelve billion kilometres from the Sun, the solar wind finally runs out of push. It has rushed out at about a million miles an hour — and at the termination shock, it suddenly slows down. Beyond it, in the heliosheath, the wind piles up, heats up and swirls, pressed back by the thin wind that blows between the stars. Then comes the heliopause — the edge of the Sun's bubble. Both Voyager spacecraft have crossed it, and they are still sending back word from interstellar space.

The Atiras are the rarest near-Earth family — only thirty-eight are known. They never leave Earth's orbit: they circle the Sun entirely inside it. That keeps them close to the Sun in our sky, so they can only be searched for in twilight, at dusk or dawn. That is why so few have been found — not because they are rare out in space. Living so close to the Sun, they are baked: one of them heats to about five hundred degrees.

The Atens go round the Sun in less than a year. Their orbits are smaller than ours, but stretched enough to cross out past Earth's path. More than three thousand are known. Most are small, a few hundred metres across, and many spin fast — Hathor turns in under three and a half hours. Two famous neighbours are Atens: Apophis, which will pass close to Earth in twenty twenty-nine, and Cruithne, which travels a horseshoe-shaped path alongside us.

The Apollos are the biggest near-Earth family — more than twenty-four thousand are known. Their orbits are bigger than Earth's, but they dip inside it, crossing our path. They come in every shape: potatoes, spinning tops, and two lumps resting together. About one in six has a little moon of its own. Two of them, Bennu and Ryugu, have been visited by spacecraft that brought pieces home — and the dust from Bennu holds some of the building blocks of life.

The Amors come close to Earth, but never cross our path — their nearest point to the Sun lies just outside Earth's orbit. Nearly fifteen thousand are known, and the biggest near-Earth asteroids are Amors: Ganymed, about thirty-eight kilometres across, Don Quixote, and Eros. Among them is a mountain of solid metal, called nineteen eighty-six DA. And Don Quixote turned out to be something else entirely — a sleeping comet.

Not every world travels alone. These two go round a shared balance point between them, as they journey round the Sun together. Pairs like this are surprisingly common. Among near-Earth asteroids, about one in six has a moon, and far beyond Neptune, many worlds come in twos. And watching them circle each other is how we weigh them.

A comet spends most of its life as a dark, frozen lump, far from the Sun. But as it falls closer, sunlight warms its ice, and the ice turns straight into gas. The gas carries dust out with it, and a glowing cloud grows around the comet — its head. Then the Sun pushes it all back into tails: a curving, golden tail of dust, and a straight blue tail of gas, blown by the solar wind. Both always point away from the Sun — even when the comet is heading back out again.

The Sun is not standing still. It is racing around the centre of our galaxy at about two hundred and thirty kilometres every second — more than eight hundred thousand kilometres every hour. And it takes everything with it: every planet, every moon, every comet. So the planets do not really move in circles. They follow the Sun in long spirals, like a corkscrew through the stars. Right now, we are all heading towards the constellation Cygnus, the Swan.

One lap around the Milky Way takes about two hundred and thirty million years. That is a galactic year. Earth is about twenty galactic years old. One lap ago, the first dinosaurs were just appearing. We live about twenty-six thousand light-years from the centre, where a black hole four million times the mass of the Sun sits hidden behind clouds of dust. And as we go round, the Sun also bobs gently up and down through the galaxy's disc, like a horse on a carousel.

Four point six billion years ago, there was no Sun — only a cold, dark cloud of gas and dust. It began to collapse under its own gravity. As it fell inwards it spun faster, and flattened into a disc. At the centre, the Sun switched on. Out in the disc, dust grains stuck together into pebbles, pebbles into boulders, boulders into small worlds — and the small worlds crashed together into planets. We can still watch this happening today, around young stars, where telescopes find flat discs of dust with dark rings carved by newborn planets.

The Sun never stops blowing. Its outer atmosphere, the corona, is over a million degrees — too hot for even the Sun's gravity to hold. So it streams away into space as a wind of charged particles, more than a million tonnes every second. Because the Sun turns as it blows, the wind is twisted into a giant spiral, like water from a spinning garden sprinkler. It flows out past every planet, until, far beyond Neptune, the wind between the stars finally stops it.

Sometimes the Sun does more than blow. Its magnetic field gets twisted up, tighter and tighter, until a loop suddenly snaps — and hurls a billion tonnes of glowing plasma into space, at up to three thousand kilometres a second. This is a coronal mass ejection. The fastest ones reach Earth in less than a day. When one hits us, our magnetic shield shakes, satellites and power grids feel it, and the northern and southern lights spread far from the poles. In May twenty twenty-four, one of these storms painted aurora across skies that almost never see them.

Some comets dive almost straight into the Sun. Most of them belong to one family, the Kreutz sungrazers — pieces of a single giant comet that broke apart centuries ago, still arriving one at a time on the same path. A spacecraft watching the Sun finds one every few days. They pass within a whisker of the Sun's surface, and almost none survive. The small ones simply boil away. In twenty thirteen, Comet ISON went in whole, and came out as dust. Often, all that is left is the glowing tail — outliving the comet that made it.

Mercury, the little planet closest to the Sun, has a tail — just like a comet. The solar wind and tiny meteorites knock sodium atoms off its surface, and then sunlight itself pushes them away, into a faint, glowing stream about twenty-four million kilometres long. It shines the same orange-yellow as old sodium street lights. And it grows strongest twice in every orbit, as Mercury speeds up and slows down near the Sun.

On the same nights every year, the sky fills with shooting stars. Comets leave trails of dust along their paths, and Earth's orbit crosses those trails at the same places each year. Each grain, often no bigger than a grain of sand, hits our air at tens of kilometres a second, and burns up in a streak of light. The Perseids each August come from comet Swift–Tuttle. The Geminids each December come from the rock Phaethon. There are about a dozen major showers a year — all you need is a dark sky, and patience.

The Sun is about four hundred times wider than our Moon — and, by pure chance, about four hundred times farther away. So from Earth, the two look almost exactly the same size. When the Moon passes exactly in front of the Sun, its shadow falls on Earth and races across it, at more than seventeen hundred kilometres an hour. Inside that shadow, day turns to night for a few minutes, the stars come out, and the Sun's glowing outer atmosphere appears around a black disc. Any one place on Earth waits, on average, about three hundred and seventy-five years to see it.

Many people think summer comes when Earth is closer to the Sun. It does not. Earth is closest to the Sun in early January — in the middle of northern winter. The seasons come from tilt. Earth's axis leans about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it keeps pointing the same way as we go round the Sun. For half the year, the north leans towards the Sun, with long days and high sunshine. For the other half, the south does. On Uranus, tipped right over on its side, each season lasts twenty-one years.

Deep inside Earth, molten iron churns, and makes a magnetic field. It surrounds our planet like an invisible shield, turning aside the solar wind that would otherwise wear away our air. When a storm from the Sun strikes, the shield is squeezed and shaken. Energy pours down near the poles, and the air there glows green and red — the aurora. Other planets have shields too. Jupiter's is so huge that, if we could see it, it would look bigger than the full Moon in our sky.

Earth's air protects us from small space rocks — they burn up as shooting stars. The Moon has no air at all. So the same little rocks, many no bigger than a pebble, hit its ground at tens of kilometres a second. All that speed turns into heat in an instant, and makes a flash of light bright enough to see from Earth through a telescope. Each flash lasts less than a tenth of a second. Watchers catch about one every hour and a half. The rocks hit the whole Moon — but we can only see the flashes on its dark side.

Mars is a dusty planet, and every Martian year brings dust storms. But every few Earth years, one of them refuses to die. Sunlight warms the dust in the air, the warm air drives stronger winds, the winds lift more dust — and the storm feeds itself, until it wraps around the entire planet. From space, the surface of Mars disappears for weeks. In twenty eighteen, one of these storms blotted out the sunlight that powered the Opportunity rover. After almost fifteen years exploring Mars, it never woke up again.

Jupiter is the Solar System's great target. Its huge gravity pulls in stray asteroids, comets and icy wanderers from every direction. Rocks just a few metres across strike it tens of times a year. They never reach the ground — Jupiter has none. They explode high in its clouds in a flash of light, and leave nothing behind. Amateur astronomers, filming Jupiter from their backyards, catch one of these flashes every year or so. And in nineteen ninety-four, the whole world watched the pieces of comet Shoemaker–Levy nine slam into Jupiter, leaving dark scars bigger than Earth.

Io is the most volcanic world we know. It has around four hundred active volcanoes, and at every moment, something is erupting. Some throw fountains of sulphur three hundred kilometres up into space, in great umbrella-shaped plumes that fall back as frost. The heat comes from Jupiter: Io is squeezed and stretched by its giant planet on every orbit. So much lava pours out that Io's whole surface is repainted every few thousand years. Its yellows, oranges and reds are not rock — they are sulphur.

Saturn gets hit too — though less often than Jupiter, perhaps a fifth as often, because it is smaller and farther out. Most of what strikes it are icy wanderers falling in from beyond Neptune. Nobody has yet confirmed seeing a flash on Saturn. But its rings remember. The Cassini spacecraft found ripples in them, left by a cloud of debris that struck them in nineteen eighty-three — when no one was watching.

About once every thirty years — once in each Saturn year — a giant storm bursts out on Saturn. For decades, heat and water vapour build up deep beneath its clouds, trapped under lighter air. Then, all at once, they break through. A bright white storm appears, and spreads along its band until it wraps right round the planet. The last one, in twenty ten, was the biggest ever recorded. It raged for months, and changed Saturn's atmosphere for years afterwards.

Look closely at Saturn's rings, and sometimes you will see ghostly spokes — dark smudges thousands of kilometres long, lying across the rings like the spokes of a wheel. They appear, and fade, within hours. They should be impossible: the inner part of the rings goes round faster than the outer part, so a straight line should be smeared away in minutes. The secret is electricity. Tiny grains of ice pick up a charge, lift off the rings, and are carried round by Saturn's magnetic field instead. They come in seasons — and right now, it is spoke season.

Enceladus is a small moon, only about five hundred kilometres across — but beneath its icy shell lies a whole ocean. Saturn's pull flexes the moon and keeps the water from freezing. At the south pole, four long cracks in the ice, nicknamed the tiger stripes, let it escape. More than a hundred jets spray water out into space. Some falls back as snow. The rest drifts away and becomes one of Saturn's rings. The Cassini spacecraft flew right through the spray and tasted it: salt, sand-like silica, and carbon-rich molecules. It is one of the best places to look for life beyond Earth.

For years, Uranus looked like a calm, blank ball. When Voyager 2 flew past in nineteen eighty-six, it saw almost nothing at all. That is because Uranus lies on its side. For decades, one pole faces the Sun while the other sits in darkness, and the weather goes quiet. But as Uranus moves along its orbit, sunlight returns to both halves, and the storms wake up. In twenty fourteen, telescopes counted eight big storms in a single night — bright enough for backyard astronomers to see.

Triton is one of the coldest places ever measured — just thirty-eight degrees above absolute zero. And yet it has geysers. Faint sunlight shines through clear nitrogen ice and warms the darker ground beneath it. The nitrogen there turns to gas, pressure builds, and it bursts out, carrying dark dust eight kilometres high. Then the thin wind catches each plume, and streams it out a hundred and fifty kilometres. Voyager 2 saw them in nineteen eighty-nine — and no spacecraft has been back since.

In twenty seventeen, astronomers spotted something strange, already on its way out of our Solar System. It had come from another star. It was moving far too fast for the Sun to hold, on a path that falls in once, swings round the Sun, and leaves for ever. They named it Oumuamua — a Hawaiian word for a scout, a messenger from far away. We never saw it as more than a point of light, flickering as it tumbled. And it sped up slightly as it left, with no gas or dust to show why. It is still a mystery.

Two years later came the second visitor from another star — and this one was a comet. It was found by Gennadiy Borisov, an amateur astronomer, using a telescope he had built himself. As the Sun warmed it, it grew a glowing head and tail, just like our own comets. But its gas was different: it held far more carbon monoxide than any comet born here. For the first time, we were tasting the ice of another solar system.

In twenty twenty-five came the third visitor — and the fastest ever recorded. Even before the Sun's pull sped it up, it was moving at fifty-eight kilometres every second — so fast that its path through our Solar System is almost a straight line. It came from the direction of the centre of our galaxy, and it may be older than the Sun itself. As it passed Mars, spacecraft there — and even a rover on the Martian ground — photographed it. Now it is heading back out to the stars, never to return.

When a giant star — at least eight times heavier than our Sun — runs out of fuel, its heart collapses in less than a second. The rest of the star crashes inwards, rebounds, and explodes. For weeks, that one dying star can outshine its whole galaxy of billions. The blast scatters the elements forged inside it — oxygen, silicon, iron — out into space, where they become part of new stars, and new planets. The oxygen you are breathing was made in stars like this. Our galaxy has about two supernovae every century, but most are hidden from us behind dust.

When a giant star explodes, it can leave behind a neutron star — a ball only about twenty kilometres across, but heavier than the Sun. A teaspoon of it would weigh as much as a mountain. Sometimes two of them circle each other, closer and closer, until they crash together. That collision is a kilonova. In a few seconds, it forges gold, platinum and other heavy elements, and flings them into space. In twenty seventeen, we saw one for the first time — and felt it too, as ripples in space itself. Much of the gold on Earth was made this way.

An A.U. — an astronomical unit — is the distance from Earth to the Sun: about a hundred and fifty million kilometres. It is the ruler we use for the Solar System. Light crosses it in just over eight minutes, and Neptune is thirty of them away.

Light travels almost three hundred thousand kilometres every second — the fastest anything can go. Fast enough to circle Earth seven and a half times in one second. And still, space is so big that light has to wait.

Light takes time to reach us, so every view is a look into the past. You see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago, and Neptune as it was four hours ago. The further you look, the older the light.

A light-year is a distance, not a time: how far light travels in one year — about nine and a half trillion kilometres. The nearest star to the Sun is just over four light-years away.

A parsec is about three and a quarter light-years. As Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift very slightly against the stars far behind it. Measuring that tiny shift is how we first found the distances to the stars.

The inner planets are the four small, rocky worlds closest to the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. They have solid ground, thin air or none at all, and hardly any moons.

The main asteroid belt is a wide ring of rocky leftovers between Mars and Jupiter. There are millions of rocks — but spread so thinly that spacecraft fly straight through without having to dodge. Jupiter's pull stopped them from ever gathering into a planet.

The giant planets — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune — are huge worlds of gas and ice, with no solid ground to stand on. Every one of them has rings, and dozens of moons.

The Kuiper Belt is a thick ring of icy worlds beyond Neptune. It is like the asteroid belt — but far wider, and made mostly of ice. Pluto lives here, along with thousands of others.

The Plutinos go round the Sun twice for every three laps of Neptune, in step with Pluto — which is where their name comes from. That rhythm means Neptune is never nearby when they cross its path, so they are never thrown out.

The classical Kuiper Belt is the belt's main crowd, on calm, nearly round orbits that Neptune never disturbed. It is so peaceful out here that many of its worlds are still in pairs, just as they formed.

The scattered disc is made of worlds that Neptune kicked, long ago, onto long, stretched, tilted orbits. They swing in close to Neptune, then out to hundreds of times Earth's distance from the Sun.

The heliosphere is the Sun's bubble — blown into space by the solar wind. Its edge, the heliopause, is where the Sun's wind meets the gas between the stars, about a hundred and twenty times Earth's distance from the Sun. Both Voyager spacecraft have crossed it.

Detached worlds never come close enough for Neptune to touch them. Something else must have lifted them out there — perhaps a passing star, or perhaps a planet we have not found yet. Sedna is the best known.

The Oort Cloud is a vast, thin shell of icy comets thought to surround the whole Solar System — reaching over a third of the way to the next star. No one has ever seen it directly. We know it is there from the comets that fall in from it.

An asteroid is a lump of rock or metal left over from when the planets formed. Most are too small to pull themselves round, so they are shaped like potatoes — from a few metres to a few hundred kilometres across.

A near-Earth asteroid is one whose orbit brings it close to Earth's own path around the Sun. These are the rocks that astronomers watch most carefully.

A Centaur is an icy body that has drifted in from beyond Neptune and now loops between the giant planets. Part asteroid, part comet — like the half-horse creature of myth. Within a few million years, a giant planet will fling it away.

A comet is a dirty snowball. Far from the Sun, it is a dark, frozen lump. Near the Sun, its ice turns to gas and streams off into a glowing tail — a tail that always points away from the Sun.

A trans-Neptunian object is anything whose orbit lies, on average, beyond Neptune. Pluto is the famous one — and thousands more are known.

A dwarf planet is big enough for its own gravity to pull it into a ball — but not big enough to clear its path of other bodies, so it shares its orbit with a crowd. The official five are Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris.

A rubble pile is not one solid rock, but a loose heap of boulders and gravel, held together only by its own weak gravity. Bennu and Ryugu, both sampled by spacecraft, are rubble piles.

On every planet's orbit, there are two special places — one sixty degrees ahead of the planet, one sixty degrees behind — where the pull of the Sun and the planet balance. A small body there can share the planet's orbit for ever. Jupiter keeps two great swarms there, named after the heroes of the Trojan War.

An interstellar object is something that formed around another star, and is only passing through. It moves too fast for the Sun to catch, so it comes in once and leaves for ever. Three are known, and you can watch each one pass, in Space events.

As a comet nears the Sun, its ice turns straight into gas, dragging dust off with it into a glowing cloud — the coma — thousands of times wider than the comet itself. Sunlight then pushes the dust back into a tail. Far out again, it is just a bare, dark rock.

A contact binary is two bodies that drifted together so gently that they stuck, touching at a narrow neck. Arrokoth, visited by New Horizons, is one.

A binary is two bodies of similar weight going round each other. The point they both circle lies in open space between them, so neither one is the other's moon. A triple has a third body circling the pair.

With a planet and its moon, one is much heavier than the other. The point they circle lies inside the planet — for Earth and the Moon, it is deep under the ground. So the planet only wobbles, and the moon does the going round.

A tidally locked moon spins exactly once in each orbit, so it always shows the same face. Our Moon does it. And some pairs, like Pluto and Charon, have locked each other — each keeping one face turned to the other.

Rings are countless pieces of ice and rock circling in one thin, flat band. Not only the giant planets have them — so do small worlds, like Chariklo, Haumea and Quaoar.

An orbit is falling, and always missing. A moon is falling towards its planet the whole time — but it moves sideways so fast that the planet's surface keeps curving away beneath it.

Eccentricity is how stretched an orbit is. Zero is a perfect circle. Close to one is long and thin, like a comet's. The more stretched it is, the more a body speeds up close in, and dawdles far out.

Retrograde means going round backwards — against the direction almost everything else in the Solar System turns. Many small moons that were captured by their planets go round this way.

Some small rocks share Earth's year, on a slightly different path. They orbit the Sun, not Earth — but seen from Earth, some seem to circle us once a year, and others creep ahead of us and behind us over the centuries, in the shape of a horseshoe.

A resonance is when two orbits keep a simple rhythm — like two laps to one, or three to two. The regular tugs add up. They can protect an orbit, like Pluto's, or empty one out, like the gaps in the asteroid belt.

Albedo is how much light a surface throws back. Soot reflects about four per cent, Earth about thirty, and fresh snow about eighty. Most worlds beyond Neptune are darker than you would think.

Tholins are a reddish-brown gunk, made when sunlight and cosmic rays break apart simple ices like methane, over millions of years. They are why Pluto's dark regions, and most of the far worlds, are red.

Beyond Earth brings the Solar System to you. Everything you see here was made by nature, not by people — no spacecraft, no satellites, no space stations. Just the Sun, the planets and their moons, the asteroids, the comets, and the icy worlds beyond Neptune: six hundred and eighty-four of them, each on its real path, where it really is today. Watch the things that really happen out here, look up any word, and ride with the Sun around the galaxy. Beyond Earth was imagined by Harsh Pandya, and brought to life with the help of A.I. Stay as long as you like. Nothing out here is in a hurry.

This is Aten, the first asteroid found with a year shorter than Earth's. The whole Aten family is named after it.

This is Atira, the first asteroid found that stays inside Earth's orbit the whole way round — and it has a moon of its own.

This is Icarus, an asteroid that swings closer to the Sun than Mercury, scorching hot, and then out past Mars.

This is Geographos, one of the most stretched-out asteroids known — nearly three times longer than it is wide.

This is Polymele, a small, flattened Trojan of Jupiter, with a moon of its own — another of Lucy's targets.

This is Leucus, one of the slowest-spinning objects known. One turn takes about four and a half days. Lucy will fly past it in twenty twenty-eight.

This is Orus, one of Jupiter's Trojans, and one of the worlds the Lucy spacecraft is on its way to visit.

This is Bienor, a stretched-out Centaur — perhaps two bodies, stuck together.
