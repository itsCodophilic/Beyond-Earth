When one of Jupiter's big moons passes between Jupiter and the Sun, its shadow falls on the cloud tops as a small, perfectly black dot. Anyone standing under it on Jupiter would see a total eclipse. Sometimes two, or even three, moon shadows cross Jupiter at the same time.

Europa is covered in ice, but underneath lies an ocean of salty water, with perhaps twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans. Jupiter's gravity squeezes and stretches Europa as it orbits, and that flexing warms the inside, keeping the ocean from freezing. It is one of the best places to look for life beyond Earth.

Jupiter has lightning, deep down in its water clouds, flashing in giant thunderstorms. The Juno spacecraft found it is most common near the poles. High above the storms, there are also brief red flashes called sprites, the same kind of upper-air lightning seen above thunderstorms on Earth.

At Saturn's north pole there is a jet stream shaped like a hexagon, with six straight sides, each one wider than the Earth. It has been there at least since the Voyager probes flew past in the early nineteen-eighties. It is thought to form where winds of different speeds meet, in a stable, wavy pattern.

Saturn's rings are hundreds of thousands of kilometres wide, but in most places only about ten metres thick. So about every fifteen years, when Earth lines up with the plane of the rings, we see them edge-on, and they almost disappear. It last happened in March twenty twenty-five.

Saturn's rings are slowly raining into the planet. Tiny ice grains become electrically charged and are pulled along the magnetic field, down into Saturn's atmosphere. The Cassini spacecraft measured this ring rain. At this rate, the rings may be mostly gone in a few hundred million years, so we may be living at the best time to see them.

Hidden in Saturn's rings are tiny moonlets, too small to see, only a few hundred metres across. As they orbit, their gravity stirs up the ring particles on either side, carving gaps shaped like aeroplane propellers. The Cassini spacecraft found many of them.

Saturn's thin F ring is guarded by two small moons, Prometheus just inside it and Pandora just outside. Their gravity herds the ring's particles and keeps the ring narrow. And every time Prometheus swings close, it pulls out streamers of ice, twisting the ring into knots and braids.

Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has weather. It is far too cold for water to flow, but methane, which is a gas on Earth, falls there as rain. It carves rivers, and fills lakes and seas near the poles, under a thick orange haze. Titan is the only place besides Earth with liquid on its surface.

Saturn's moon Hyperion looks like a giant sponge, full of deep craters, because it is mostly empty space inside. And it does not spin in any regular way. Tugged by Saturn's big moon Titan, it tumbles chaotically, so its spin can never be predicted for long.

Iapetus is a moon with two faces. The side that leads as it orbits is as dark as coal. The other side is as bright as snow. Dark dust swept up from farther out darkens the leading side, which then warms in the sunlight, and its ice moves away to the bright side, making the difference even starker.

Uranus spins on its side. Its axis is tipped over by ninety-eight degrees, so it rolls around the Sun like a ball. The most likely explanation is a giant collision long ago, with a world several times the size of Earth, that knocked the young planet over.

Because Uranus lies on its side, its seasons are extreme. During each eighty-four-year orbit, one pole points almost straight at the Sun for decades, while the other is in darkness. So each pole gets about forty-two years of sunlight, then forty-two years of night.

In nineteen eighty-nine, the Voyager Two spacecraft found a dark storm on Neptune as big as the Earth, which became known as the Great Dark Spot. When the Hubble Space Telescope looked in nineteen ninety-four, it was gone. Neptune's storms come and go within a few years.

Neptune has the fastest winds in the Solar System, about two thousand kilometres an hour, faster than the speed of sound on Earth. And it is the planet farthest from the Sun, getting the least sunlight of all. Where the energy for such winds comes from is still a puzzle.

Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, the pressure is millions of times greater than on Earth. There, carbon from methane may be squeezed into diamonds, which slowly sink toward the core, like hail. Experiments with powerful lasers on Earth have made tiny diamonds in the same way.

Triton, Neptune's largest moon, orbits backwards, against Neptune's spin. That means it was probably captured from the Kuiper Belt, long ago. Neptune's tides are slowly drawing it inward, and in a few billion years, it will be torn apart, and may become a ring.

Pluto and its moon Charon are so close in size that they are almost a double world. They are locked face to face, each always showing the other the same side. And they both circle a point in the empty space between them, so Pluto wobbles as Charon goes round.

Charon, Pluto's largest moon, has a red cap on its north pole. Gas slowly escapes from Pluto's atmosphere, and some of it is caught by Charon. In the long polar winter it freezes onto the pole, and when sunlight returns, it slowly turns the ice reddish brown.

Arrokoth, far beyond Pluto, is made of two small worlds stuck together, like a cosmic snowman. Billions of years ago, they circled each other, slowly drew together, and touched at walking pace, so gently that neither was broken. It shows how the very first building blocks of planets came together.

In about one point three million years, a star called Gliese seven-ten will pass through the outer edge of our Solar System, about ten thousand times farther away than the Sun is from Earth. Its gravity will disturb the Oort Cloud, and send a shower of comets drifting inward over the following million years.

Halley's Comet comes back about every seventy-six years. It was seen by ancient astronomers, and was woven into the Bayeux Tapestry in ten sixty-six. Edmond Halley predicted its return, and it came back, as he said, after his death. It will next be at its closest to the Sun in the summer of twenty sixty-one.
