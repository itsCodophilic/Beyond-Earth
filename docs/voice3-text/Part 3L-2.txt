In a total lunar eclipse, the Moon slides into Earth's shadow. But it does not disappear. It turns a deep coppery red, because Earth's atmosphere bends a little sunlight into the shadow. You are seeing the light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth, all at once, falling on the Moon.

The Moon's orbit is an oval, so sometimes it is a little farther from us, and looks a little smaller. If an eclipse happens then, the Moon cannot cover the whole Sun. It leaves a blazing ring of fire around its edge. That is an annular eclipse, from the Latin word for a ring.

On the second of August twenty twenty-seven, the Moon's shadow will sweep across North Africa and the Middle East. Over Luxor, in Egypt, the Sun will be completely hidden for more than six minutes, one of the longest total eclipses of the century. The Moon will be near its closest to Earth, and Earth near its farthest from the Sun.

The Moon's distance from us changes over every orbit. A full Moon near its closest point is called a supermoon. It looks about fourteen percent bigger and thirty percent brighter than a full Moon at its farthest. Side by side, the difference is clear, even though by eye it is hard to tell.

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. What changes is how much of that lit half faces us. As the Moon circles Earth each month, we see a thin crescent, then a half, then a full Moon, and back again. The phases are just the Moon's day and night, seen from here.

The Moon and the Sun both pull on Earth's oceans, raising two bulges of water on opposite sides of the planet. As Earth turns through them, the tide comes in and goes out about twice a day. When the Sun and Moon line up, we get the highest tides of all, spring tides. When they pull at right angles, the tides are gentle, neap tides.

The Moon always shows us the same face. That is not a coincidence. Long ago, Earth's pull on the Moon slowed its spin, until it turned exactly once for every trip around us. No one saw the far side until a spacecraft photographed it in nineteen fifty-nine. Most of the big moons in the Solar System are locked the same way.

The Moon does not hold perfectly still. Its oval orbit, and its slight tilt, make it seem to rock from side to side and nod up and down over a month. Thanks to that wobble, we can see a little way around its edges, about fifty-nine percent of its surface in all, not just half.

Look at a thin crescent Moon on a clear evening, and you can often see the rest of it, glowing faintly. That glow is earthshine. It is sunlight that has bounced off the Earth, travelled to the Moon, and bounced back to your eyes. Leonardo da Vinci was the first to explain it, five hundred years ago.

Our Moon was probably born in a collision. About four and a half billion years ago, a world about the size of Mars, often called Theia, smashed into the young Earth. The impact threw a ring of molten rock into orbit, and within a short time, it gathered itself into the Moon.

The Moon still quakes. Instruments left by the Apollo astronauts recorded thousands of moonquakes. Most are tiny, but shallow ones can reach magnitude five. And because the Moon is so dry and cold, it does not damp the shaking. The whole Moon can ring like a bell for many minutes.

Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about ten kilometres wide struck what is now the coast of Mexico. It blasted a crater a hundred and eighty kilometres across, and threw so much dust and soot into the sky that the Sun was dimmed for years. Three quarters of all species died out, including every dinosaur that could not fly.

On the thirtieth of June nineteen-oh-eight, a space rock exploded in the air above Siberia. The blast flattened about eighty million trees, across more than two thousand square kilometres of forest. But it left no crater, because it burst a few kilometres up, before it ever reached the ground.

On the fifteenth of February twenty thirteen, a rock about twenty metres wide streaked across the morning sky over Russia, briefly brighter than the Sun. It burst about thirty kilometres up. The shock wave arrived minutes later, shattering windows across the city of Chelyabinsk. No telescope had seen it coming.

On the tenth of August nineteen seventy-two, a space rock several metres across skimmed through Earth's upper atmosphere over the western United States and Canada. Hundreds of people saw it blaze across the daytime sky. Then, like a stone skipping on water, it bounced back out into space, and carried on around the Sun.

On Friday the thirteenth of April twenty twenty-nine, the asteroid Apophis, about three hundred and forty metres wide, will pass about thirty-two thousand kilometres from Earth's surface. That is closer than some satellites. It will be bright enough to see without a telescope, and we are now sure it will miss.

In the autumn of twenty twenty-four, Earth briefly had a second moon. A small asteroid, only about ten metres across, drifted slowly enough to be caught by Earth's gravity in a loose loop. It stayed for about two months, then drifted away again, back into orbit around the Sun.

On the night of the twelfth of November eighteen thirty-three, the sky over North America filled with shooting stars, tens of thousands an hour, falling like snow. Earth had ploughed through a dense trail of dust left by Comet Tempel–Tuttle. The study of meteors as a science began that night.

Earth's axis wobbles, like a spinning top that is slowing down, tracing a slow circle in the sky once every twenty-six thousand years. Today our axis points at Polaris, the North Star. In about twelve thousand years, it will point near Vega, and Vega will be our pole star.

Earth's magnetic field is made by molten iron churning in the core, and every so often it flips. North becomes south. We can read the record in old lava flows, which froze with the field of their time locked inside. The last full reversal was about seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago.

STEVE is a narrow ribbon of purple light that sometimes appears in the sky, below the aurora. It was named by aurora watchers, and later given a scientific name to fit. It is not an aurora at all. It glows from a river of very hot gas, rushing westward high above the ground.

On dark, clear nights, just after sunset or before dawn, a faint cone of light rises from the horizon along the path of the planets. It is zodiacal light: sunlight, scattered by countless grains of dust drifting between the planets, much of it shed by comets and asteroids.
