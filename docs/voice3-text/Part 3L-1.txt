Here is a fact that sounds impossible. Line up every other planet, Mercury to Neptune, edge to edge, and together they measure about three hundred and eighty thousand kilometres. That is almost exactly the distance to the Moon. At its average distance they overshoot by a whisker, but when the Moon swings out to its farthest, they all fit, with room to spare.

In most pictures, the Moon looks like it is just next door. It is not. You could line up about thirty Earths, side by side, in the gap between us and the Moon. Light crosses that gap in just over a second. The Apollo astronauts took three days.

This is the Sun next to the Earth, drawn to scale. Earth is that tiny blue dot, and the Sun is a hundred and nine Earths wide. But fill it up, and it could hold about one point three million Earths. The Sun alone holds almost all the mass of the Solar System.

Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a single storm, wider than the whole Earth. It has been raging for at least two hundred years, with winds faster than four hundred kilometres an hour. But it is shrinking. A hundred and forty years ago, three Earths could have fitted across it. Today, only about one and a third.

Saturn is almost ten Earths wide, and yet it is so light for its size that, in a big enough bathtub, it would float. It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the lightest things there are, so its average density is less than water's. Earth, made of rock and iron, would sink straight to the bottom.

On Venus, a day is longer than a year. It takes two hundred and twenty-five Earth days to go once around the Sun, but two hundred and forty-three to turn once on its axis. And it spins backwards, so there the Sun rises in the west. From one sunrise to the next takes about a hundred and seventeen Earth days.

This is Everest, almost nine kilometres high, the tallest mountain on Earth. And this is Olympus Mons, on Mars: about twenty-two kilometres high, two and a half Everests, and as wide as France. Mars has no moving plates, so its volcanoes sat over the same hot spot for ages, and just kept growing.

Mars has a canyon that would stretch right across the United States. Valles Marineris is about four thousand kilometres long, and in places seven kilometres deep. Our Grand Canyon would fit inside one of its side branches. It was not carved by a river. As Mars's giant volcanoes swelled up beside it, the ground split open.

The Moon is slowly drifting away from us, about four centimetres every year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. We know, because astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and we bounce lasers off them. The tides the Moon raises on Earth pull it gently outward, and slow our spin. Long ago, a day on Earth lasted only about eighteen hours.

When you look at the Sun, you are seeing the past. Its light takes eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. To reach Jupiter, it takes forty-three minutes, and to reach Neptune, more than four hours. Watch one flash leave the Sun, and race outward past every planet.

The light on your face left the Sun eight minutes ago. But that is only the end of its journey. It was born deep in the core, then spent tens of thousands of years bouncing its way out, absorbed and thrown off again in random directions, over and over. Some of today's sunlight began that trip before the first farms on Earth.

Jupiter is the heavyweight. Put it on one side of a cosmic balance, and every other planet on the other side. Jupiter still wins. It is two and a half times heavier than all of them combined, because it formed first, and grabbed most of the gas left over from the birth of the Sun.

A solar flare is a sudden flash of light on the Sun. Twisted magnetic fields above a group of sunspots snap and reconnect, releasing in minutes as much energy as billions of nuclear bombs. Its light reaches Earth in eight minutes, before anything else, and the biggest flares can black out radio on the day side of the planet.

In September eighteen fifty-nine, the biggest solar storm on record struck Earth. The night before, the astronomer Richard Carrington had watched a brilliant flare on the Sun. Less than a day later, auroras lit up skies near the tropics, bright enough to read by, and telegraph wires sparked and carried messages with their batteries unplugged.

In May twenty twenty-four, a burst of eruptions from one huge group of sunspots hit Earth one after another. It was the strongest geomagnetic storm in more than twenty years. Auroras were photographed as far south as India, Mexico and the Canary Islands, and millions of people saw the northern lights for the first time in their lives.

The Sun has a heartbeat about eleven years long. At solar minimum it is calm and almost spotless. At solar maximum it is covered in sunspots, flares and eruptions. And at every maximum, its magnetic north and south poles swap places. The cycle that began in twenty nineteen peaked around the end of twenty twenty-four.

A prominence is a giant loop of glowing gas, many times taller than the Earth, held up above the Sun's surface by its magnetic field. Some hang quietly for weeks. Others become unstable and erupt, flinging billions of tonnes of plasma out into space.

Coronal holes are dark patches in the Sun's outer atmosphere. Here the magnetic field does not loop back down, but opens straight out into space, so hot gas escapes freely, as the fastest solar wind, at around eight hundred kilometres a second. When one faces Earth, auroras often follow a few days later.

The Sun is not solid, so it does not turn as one piece. Its equator spins once in about twenty-five days, but near the poles a turn takes about thirty-five. This uneven spin winds up the Sun's magnetic field like a spring, and that is what drives the sunspots, the flares and the whole eleven-year cycle.

Up close, the Sun's surface is boiling. Each bright cell is a column of hot gas the size of a country, rising from below, cooling, and sinking again at its dark edges. A single cell lasts only about ten minutes, and there are around four million of them on the Sun at any moment.

The Sun's core makes more than light. It also fires out neutrinos, ghostly particles that barely touch anything. Every second, tens of billions of them pass through your thumbnail, and straight through the whole Earth, day and night. Catching even a few of them takes a giant tank deep underground.

The Sun will not last forever. In about five billion years, it will run short of hydrogen in its core and swell into a red giant, swallowing Mercury and Venus, and maybe Earth. Then it will puff off its outer layers as a glowing cloud, and shrink into a white dwarf, about the size of the Earth.
