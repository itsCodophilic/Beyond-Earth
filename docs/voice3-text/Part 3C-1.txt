This is Vesta, the brightest asteroid — sometimes you can see it without a telescope. A giant impact near its south pole blasted out pieces that still fall to Earth as meteorites.

This is Pallas, one of the biggest asteroids, on an orbit tilted steeply to all the others — a world that refuses to stay in line.

This is Hygiea, a large, dark asteroid, so round that it may be a dwarf planet itself.

This is Psyche, an asteroid rich in metal — perhaps part of the core of a world that never finished forming. A spacecraft of the same name is on its way to see it.

This is Kleopatra, a metal asteroid shaped like a dog's bone, with two little moons of its own.

This is Sylvia, the first asteroid found with two moons — a little system of three.

This is Interamnia, one of the largest asteroids of all — and no spacecraft has ever visited it.

This is Bennu, a loose pile of rubble shaped like a spinning top. A spacecraft touched it, grabbed a sample and brought it home to Earth.

This is Ryugu, another spinning-top rubble pile. A Japanese spacecraft brought back pieces of it — about fifty times more than it planned.

This is Itokawa, a little asteroid shaped like a peanut — two piles of gravel resting together, with lots of empty space inside.

This is Eros, the first asteroid a spacecraft ever orbited — and then landed on.

This is Apophis. On the thirteenth of April, twenty twenty-nine, it will pass closer to Earth than many of our satellites — close enough to see without a telescope. It will not hit us.

This is Didymos, and the little moon going round it, Dimorphos. In twenty twenty-two, a spacecraft crashed into the moon on purpose — the first time people ever changed the path of another world.

This is Arrokoth, the most distant object a spacecraft has ever visited. It is two flattened lumps that touched gently and stuck — like a snowman made in space.

This is Ida, the first asteroid ever found to have a moon of its own — little Dactyl.

This is Gaspra, the first asteroid ever photographed up close, by a spacecraft on its way to Jupiter.

This is Mathilde, a dark asteroid so light for its size that it must be full of holes.

This is Lutetia, one of the densest asteroids ever measured — far heavier than it looks.

This is Phaethon, a rock that behaves like a comet. Every December, Earth runs through the dust it leaves behind — and we see it as the Geminid meteor shower.

This is Cruithne, sometimes called Earth's second moon — but it is not a moon. It circles the Sun, and only seems to loop around us.
