This is Kamooalewa, a little rock that travels alongside Earth around the Sun — perhaps a piece of our own Moon, blasted off long ago.

This is Toutatis, an asteroid that does not spin neatly — it tumbles end over end as it goes.

This is Ganymed — not the moon, but the largest of the asteroids that come near Earth, about thirty-eight kilometres long.

This is the asteroid that gets closer to the Sun than any other known — its surface heats to about five hundred degrees, and its year lasts just a hundred and thirteen days.

This is Hektor, the largest of Jupiter's Trojans — two lumps joined together, travelling ahead of Jupiter on its own orbit.

This is Patroclus and its partner, Menoetius — two almost equal worlds circling each other, trailing behind Jupiter.

This is Hilda, which gives its name to a family of asteroids that keep a perfect rhythm with Jupiter — three laps for every two of Jupiter's.

This is Chariklo, a small icy world between the giant planets — and the first thing that is not a planet found to have rings.

This is Chiron. When it was found, it was called an asteroid — then it grew a glowing cloud like a comet. It turned out to be both.

This is Pholus, one of the reddest objects ever measured in the Solar System.

This is Echeclus, a quiet icy world that once suddenly flared up, hundreds of times brighter than normal.

This is Halley's Comet, the most famous comet of all. It comes back about every seventy-six years — next in twenty sixty-one.

This is Hale-Bopp, the great comet of nineteen ninety-seven. People could see it without a telescope for a year and a half.

This is comet NEOWISE, which lit up the summer sky in twenty twenty — the brightest comet in the northern sky in more than twenty years.

This is the comet a spacecraft followed for two whole years — and even landed on. It is shaped like a rubber duck.

This is the largest comet nucleus ever found — over a hundred kilometres across — falling in from the Oort Cloud, at the edge of the Solar System.

This is Comet Encke, which comes back more often than any other bright comet — every three point three years.

This is Pons-Brooks, nicknamed the devil comet, because its outbursts once gave it what looked like a pair of horns.

This is Tempel 1, the comet a spacecraft hit on purpose, to see what lies beneath its surface.

This is Wild 2, the first comet whose dust was caught and brought back to Earth.
