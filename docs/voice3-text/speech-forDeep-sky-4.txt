In the constellation of Cassiopeia, long-exposure photographs show a glowing heart. The Heart Nebula is a cloud of hydrogen about two hundred light-years across, seven thousand five hundred light-years away. At its centre is a cluster of young stars, some fifty times the mass of the Sun, whose fierce ultraviolet light makes the gas glow red, and whose winds carve out its shape. But the heart is a matter of where we stand. Fly round it, and the heart disappears. From another star, it would look like something else entirely.

Every faint smudge in a deep photograph may be a whole galaxy. In nineteen twenty-six, Edwin Hubble sorted them by shape. Spirals, like the Whirlpool Galaxy, are flat discs of stars, gas and dust, with arms where new stars are being born. Barred spirals have a bar of stars across the middle, and our own Milky Way is one of them. Ellipticals, like the giant M eighty-seven, are smooth balls of old stars, often made when galaxies merge. Lenticulars are discs that have run out of gas, with no arms. And irregulars, like the Large Magellanic Cloud, have been pulled out of shape. Astronomers now think the observable universe holds about two trillion galaxies.

In two thousand and seven, a young star in Centaurus called J fourteen oh seven flickered and nearly vanished, over and over, for fifty-six days. Something was passing in front of it. The best explanation is a ring system, round an unseen body, about a hundred and eighty million kilometres across: six hundred and forty times larger than Saturn's rings. Put around Saturn, rings like that would look bigger than the full Moon in our sky. There may be thirty-seven rings, with a gap that a moon could have cleared. The ringed object is called J fourteen oh seven b, and in twenty twenty-four astronomers may have spotted it, drifting free in space.

Just below Orion's Belt, beside the star Alnitak, is one of the most famous shapes in the sky: the Horsehead Nebula, about thirteen hundred and seventy-five light-years away. Behind it, a wall of hydrogen glows red, lit by the hot star Sigma Orionis. In front, a pillar of cold dust, about three and a half light-years tall, blocks that glow, and we see its silhouette: a horse's head. It was found in eighteen eighty-eight, on a photographic plate, by Williamina Fleming at Harvard. Ultraviolet light is slowly eating it away; in about five million years, it will be gone. This is the Horsehead, as the Euclid space telescope saw it.

In twenty thirteen, the Pan-STARRS telescope in Hawaii found a faint red object about eighty light-years away that is not a star. PSO J three eighteen point five minus twenty-two is a planet, about eight times the mass of Jupiter, drifting through space all on its own, with no star to circle. It is only about twenty-three million years old, so it still glows with the heat of its birth. It did not come from our Solar System. It was born far from here, either around another star, and flung out by a close pass with another planet, or alone, from a small collapsing cloud of gas, like a star that never grew big enough to shine. There may be billions of planets like it, wandering between the stars.

In March eighteen forty-three, a star in the southern constellation of Carina flared up until it was the second brightest star in the whole sky, after Sirius. Eta Carinae, seven thousand five hundred light-years away, is a pair of enormous stars, the larger about a hundred times the mass of the Sun, near the limit of how heavy a star can be. In its Great Eruption, it threw off about ten Suns' worth of gas, and yet it survived. The gas formed two huge lobes, called the Homunculus, still flying apart today. Dust from the eruption hid the star for a century. Eta Carinae may one day explode for real, as one of the brightest supernovae ever seen from Earth.

In nineteen ninety-five, the Hubble Space Telescope photographed three towering columns of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula, six thousand five hundred light-years away. They became known as the Pillars of Creation. The tallest is about four light-years long. Hot young stars nearby are boiling the cloud away with their ultraviolet light. The pillars are what is left, shielded behind denser knots at their tips. Inside those knots, new stars are forming. In twenty twenty-two, the Webb telescope looked again, seeing through the dust to the newborn stars inside.
