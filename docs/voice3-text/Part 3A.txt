One star, eight planets — and at least five dwarf planets, with more found every few years.

All of it condensed out of one collapsing cloud of gas and dust, four point six billion years ago.

You are ten light-years out, looking back in.

Before you set off, take the short tour. It shows you how to fly, how to find any world on the board, the dictionary of space words, the real events that happen out here, and the view from the Milky Way. Or skip it, and explore on your own.

This is the Sun, our star. More than a million Earths could fit inside it, and it holds almost all the mass of everything you will see here. Every planet is still falling around it.

This is Mercury, the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. Its year lasts just eighty-eight days — yet one day, from sunrise to sunrise, lasts a hundred and seventy-six.

This is Venus, the hottest planet of all — even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. Its thick clouds trap the heat, hot enough to melt lead. And it spins backwards, so here the Sun rises in the west.

This is Earth. Our home — the only place we know of where life exists. Oceans cover most of it, and a thin blue layer of air keeps every one of us alive.

This is the Moon, Earth's companion, and the fifth largest moon in the Solar System. It always shows us the same face — and it is slowly drifting away, about four centimetres every year.

This is Mars, the red planet — red because its dust is full of rust. It holds the tallest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, about two and a half times the height of Everest.

This is Jupiter, the giant — more than twice the mass of all the other planets put together. Its gravity catches and flings away many comets and asteroids, which is why it is often called Earth's shield. And its Great Red Spot is a storm bigger than our whole planet.

This is Saturn, the jewel of the Solar System. Its rings are made of countless pieces of ice, some as small as grains of sand, some as big as houses. And the planet itself is so light, it would float in a bathtub big enough to hold it.

This is Uranus, the planet that rolls around the Sun on its side. Something huge knocked it over long ago — so each pole gets forty-two years of sunlight, then forty-two years of night.

This is Neptune, the farthest planet — so far that sunlight takes four hours to reach it. It has the fastest winds in the Solar System, more than two thousand kilometres an hour.
